Trump May End Iran War Without a Nuclear Deal

Iran's escalating price for reopening the Strait of Hormuz, a 30 percent jump in US gas prices, and a midterm clock now stalking the White House are reshaping what "winning" the Iran war even means for Trump.

Sagar Kashyap · 12 Aug 2026

President Trump has privately told senior aides he is prepared to walk away from the six-month war with Iran without securing a formal nuclear agreement, provided Tehran fully reopens the Strait of Hormuz to commercial shipping, according to a Wall Street Journal report citing US officials. The disclosure marks a quiet but significant downgrade of the war's original justification: denuclearizing Iran was the stated reason for launching military action in February. Ending the war on the strength of a reopened shipping lane instead would mean the administration is prepared to call a partial outcome a win.

That plan hit turbulence almost immediately. A day before the WSJ report, Iran issued its toughest terms yet for reopening the strait, tying the waterway's fate to a list of demands that go well beyond shipping logistics.

Why Trump Is Willing to Settle for Less

According to the officials cited by WSJ, the administration considers its military objectives against Iran already complete. A White House official told the Journal that Washington has "completed all of its military objectives against Iran" and is now focused on restoring energy flows through Hormuz, while keeping military options available should Iran target ships again.

Trump's own private reasoning, as relayed to aides, rests on three pillars.

The infrastructure is already gone. Trump has told aides that Tehran is "likely unable to revive its nuclear work" after Operation Midnight Hammer inflicted heavy damage on Iran's nuclear infrastructure in June 2025.

Deterrence can substitute for a treaty. The president believes US intelligence would detect any covert Iranian rebuilding effort, and is confident that the threat of renewed strikes will serve as "an enduring deterrent" on its own, removing the need for a signed agreement to enforce compliance.

Patience has a price ceiling. Officials describe Trump as willing to ride out the current diplomatic impasse "so long as gas prices remain where they are," a condition that ties his strategic patience directly to a domestic economic indicator rather than to developments on the ground in Iran.

Publicly, Trump has downplayed the urgency. He told Axios he is "low-keying" the negotiations and "only semi-negotiating" with Tehran, adding that the US is "just watching Iran with its huge inflation and the fact they have no money." Iranian state media, meanwhile, has denied that formal negotiations are underway at all, a discrepancy that itself signals how far apart public posture and private planning have drifted on both sides.

Iran's Six Conditions for Reopening Hormuz

The complication for Trump's exit plan surfaced on August 8, when Mohammad Bagher Zolghadr, Secretary of Iran's Supreme National Security Council, laid out six conditions Washington must meet before Hormuz reopens fully. Zolghadr said the demands reflected 160 days of sustained public pressure and battlefield resolve, and that the Security Council "will never back down, neither in war nor in negotiations."

The six conditions: end all threats and insults against Iran, including any threats to the country's national and religious sanctities; permanently end the war and aggression against Iran and its allies in Lebanon, Palestine, Yemen and Iraq; lift the naval blockade and withdraw US military forces, including naval and air forces, from around Iran; fully compensate Iran for damages caused by what Tehran calls "two wars of aggression and imposition"; lift all US sanctions on Iran; and unconditionally release Iran's frozen and seized assets.

Iran has since reinforced the hard line across multiple power centers rather than softening it. Iran's Army called the current arrangements governing the strait "irreversible" and warned that any aggressor would face far higher costs than in past confrontations, while the Revolutionary Guard Corps separately insisted that Hormuz's reopening depends solely on Iran's own conditions and has nothing to do with parallel technical talks with Oman over managing shipping routes.

Iran's hardline demands are making it "increasingly difficult for the president to manufacture a face-saving off-ramp from the conflict."

That assessment comes from Mona Yacoubian, director of the Middle East program at CSIS, speaking to WSJ.

Gas Prices and the Midterm Clock

The domestic pressure point behind Trump's patience is gasoline, and the number is not small. WSJ's own reporting put the US national average at $4.02 a gallon as of the Saturday before its report, compared with $3.16 a year earlier, a roughly 27 to 30 percent increase depending on the exact week measured. Separate AAA data shows the average touching $4.06 to $4.21 across early August, with the price crossing $4 a gallon in April for the first time since August 2022.

Gas is the most visible recurring cost signal for American consumers, tracked far more closely than inflation indices or trade data, which makes it a uniquely potent political liability. Brent crude has moved in tandem, settling near $83.55 a barrel in early August as traders watched for clarity on Hormuz. With midterms now under three months away, and polling showing most Americans oppose the war and blame Trump for rising prices at the pump, the White House's stated strategy amounts to betting that a manageable gas price matters more to voters than an unresolved nuclear question.

That bet is not guaranteed to hold. Iran's escalating demands mean the standoff could drag longer than Washington's original timeline assumed, and a prolonged closure keeps shipping insurance costs and the broader Gulf risk premium elevated regardless of who eventually blinks.

What a Midterm Loss Would Mean for Trump's Administration

Republicans currently hold narrow majorities in both the House and Senate, and 2026 is widely viewed as a referendum on Trump's handling of the economy and the war. Historical precedent weighs heavily against the president's party: the party in the White House has lost House seats in nearly every midterm since the 1930s, and Republicans' current margin is thin enough that a modest swing could flip control.

If Democrats win the House, the practical consequences would be immediate and structural rather than symbolic.

Committee chairmanships would flip, handing Democrats subpoena power and control over investigative hearings into the administration's Iran war conduct, economic policy, and other matters.

Trump's legislative agenda would effectively stall. House Speaker Mike Johnson has said losing the chamber "would be the end of the Trump presidency in real effect," since a divided Congress would block further legislative wins for the remainder of the term.

A third impeachment becomes plausible, though conviction remains unlikely. Trump himself has predicted that Democrats would "find a reason" to impeach him if Republicans lose the midterms. Even so, conviction requires a two-thirds Senate supermajority, a threshold Trump's two prior impeachments never came close to reaching, and Republicans are considered more likely to hold the Senate than the House.

A pivot toward executive action. Some of Trump's own advisers have suggested he is relatively unbothered by the prospect of losing Congress, expecting to govern more through executive authority, pardons, and influence over the 2028 Republican field rather than through legislation.

In short, a midterm loss would not remove Trump from office, but it would end the legislative phase of his presidency and expose the administration, including its Iran war decisions, to sustained congressional scrutiny for the first time in his second term.

Where Things Stand as of August 12

As of today, talks between Washington and Tehran show no sign of resuming in earnest. A senior Iranian source told Reuters this week that there are no discussions underway with the US on extending the ceasefire, stating plainly that Tehran sees "nothing to extend." Trump, for his part, said Sunday that the two sides were only "semi-negotiating," and on August 10 he added a fresh precondition of his own, saying he would require compensation from Iran before talks could meaningfully resume, a demand that further narrowed the odds of a near-term breakthrough.

The one track still showing modest movement is the narrower, Oman-mediated discussion over safe shipping corridors through Hormuz, which Iranian officials have described as nearing completion. But Tehran has been explicit and consistent that an Oman shipping arrangement is not the same as a full reopening of the strait, which it maintains is contingent on the six broader conditions delivered on August 8. The war is now in its sixth month, and neither side is signaling movement toward the other's core terms.

Sourcing note: this piece draws on reporting from the Wall Street Journal, Axios, Reuters, AAA fuel-price data, and public statements from Iranian officials carried by state media, attributed as such throughout.
