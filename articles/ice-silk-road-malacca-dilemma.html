The Golden Waterway

China built a shortcut around the world's most dangerous chokepoints. It only works four months a year, and Russia holds the keys.

Sagar Kashyap · 21 Aug 2026

On the evening of August 15, a container ship called Dubai Tower slipped out of Ningbo-Zhoushan port and turned north. Not south, toward Singapore and the Strait of Malacca, the way virtually every Chinese export ship has turned for the last three decades. North, toward the Bering Strait, toward Russia's Arctic coastline, toward a route that Chinese state media has taken to calling the Golden Waterway.

By the time it docks in Felixstowe in early September, the Dubai Tower will have spent roughly 20 days at sea. The conventional route to the same British port, through the Strait of Malacca, the Indian Ocean, the Red Sea and the Suez Canal, takes about 40. Around the Cape of Good Hope, the detour most container lines have been forced into since Houthi militants started firing on Red Sea shipping in late 2023, takes closer to 50.

Chinese officials are not being subtle about what this is supposed to mean. A banner at Ningbo-Zhoushan port read: "Warmly Congratulate the first annual voyage of the China-Europe Arctic Container Route." Within days, an American congressional commission staff report was warning that the voyage handed Beijing "greater access to the Arctic" on the back of a Russia that has grown steadily more dependent on Chinese money and Chinese buyers since 2022.

The story being told, in Beijing and increasingly in Western coverage, is that China has found its way around the world's most dangerous stretches of water: the Strait of Hormuz, still effectively blockaded since February; the Bab-el-Mandeb, patrolled by Houthi missiles; and the Strait of Malacca, the 1.7-nautical-mile-wide channel that India, the United States and their allies have long understood they could close in a crisis.

The numbers tell a much narrower story. And the narrowness is the story.

The route that isn't a replacement

Start with what the Ice Silk Road actually is. It is the Northern Sea Route, a Russian-administered shipping corridor that runs along Russia's Arctic coastline from the Bering Strait in the east to Murmansk and the Barents Sea in the west. Sea Legend, the Chinese operator running the Dubai Tower and seven other vessels this season, has branded its version the China-Europe Arctic Express, or CAX.

The commercial case is real, as far as it goes. Sea Legend's own comparison: roughly 20 days to Felixstowe via the Arctic, versus roughly 40 days via Suez and roughly 50 around the Cape. That is close to a halving of transit time, and for certain cargo, electric vehicles, lithium batteries, solar panels, high-value electronics, time is money in a way that changes shipping decisions.

But look at what "regular service" means in practice. Sea Legend's 2026 season runs eight sailings, from August 15 to October 3, using seven small and mid-sized container ships. The lead vessel, Dubai Tower, carries 1,740 TEU. A modern mainline container ship serving the Asia-Europe trade carries anywhere from 15,000 to 24,000 TEU. Total Asia-Europe container trade runs to roughly 19.6 million TEU a year. Eight Arctic sailings on ships this size come to a rounding error, under one-tenth of one percent of that volume, by independent shipping-industry estimates.

Xeneta's chief analyst, Peter Sand, put the scale question bluntly when the 2025 transit numbers came in: "The number of ships transiting the Northern Sea Route last year was a record high. But we counted 15 ships, so that's what a record high looks like." He added the caveat that matters most for anyone pricing the route: "Shorter distance is clearly attractive. But you still have to factor in expensive transit costs."

Total cargo carried across the entire Northern Sea Route, not just the China-Europe container service but everything, oil, gas, ore, all of it, was 37.02 million tonnes in 2025. That was down from 37.9 million tonnes in 2024, the second consecutive year of decline. Russia's own 2018 target had called for 80 million tonnes by 2024. It missed by more than half. Transit cargo, meaning ships passing through rather than serving Russian domestic projects, came to just 3.2 million tonnes in 2025, under 9 percent of total route traffic. The Suez Canal, by comparison, carries roughly 1.6 billion tonnes of cargo a year. The Arctic route's entire annual throughput is about 2 percent of that.

Container transits specifically climbed from 7 in 2023 to somewhere between 15 and 24 in 2025, depending on whose count you use, CHNL's independent tally versus the Financial Times' broader figure. Either way, the absolute numbers are still small enough to count on your fingers and toes.

Four months, and still not alone

The deeper limitation is not just volume. It is time and control.

The Northern Sea Route is navigable for ordinary commercial vessels roughly from July through October. Sea Legend chose October 3 as its final departure date for a specific reason: it is, in the words of shipping analyst Niels Rasmussen, "broadly aligned with the period when ships can expect to transit without icebreaker assistance." Outside that window, the ice closes back in.

Even inside the window, no ship simply sails through. Russia's state nuclear corporation, Rosatom, was appointed by the Russian government to administer the entire route. It has divided the NSR into 28 separate sections, each requiring its own transit permit. Ships are monitored throughout, and icebreaker escort is available, and sometimes necessary, even during the summer season. Rosatom completed issuing permits for the 2026 season only days before Sea Legend's first sailing.

That means the Ice Silk Road is not simply seasonal. It is seasonal and permanently dependent on Russian infrastructure, Russian satellite data, Russian search-and-rescue capacity and Russian icebreakers. China has no vessel of its own capable of running this route unescorted. Russia operates eight nuclear-powered icebreakers, the only fleet of its kind in the world, deployed simultaneously for the first time in November 2025 amid an unusually early freeze. China's icebreaker fleet consists of a handful of research vessels, led by the Xue Long 2, and none of them are nuclear-powered or built for heavy commercial escort work.

Insurance reflects the same dependency. Premiums for Arctic transits run five to ten times higher than conventional coverage, and Western insurers have grown warier still given the risk that a stranded vessel in Arctic waters, under sanctions, could complicate any rescue operation, a concern flagged explicitly in Allianz Commercial's 2026 Safety and Shipping Review. Most Western carriers have simply declined to use the route at all. CHNL's managing director, Kjell Stokvik, summed up who is actually left running it: "China and Russia now are the only ones who are really using this route now."

The part the map settles

Here is the argument that ends the debate for anyone tracking India's strategic position, and it is not really a data argument. It is a geography argument.

The Northern Sea Route runs from the Bering Strait, on China's Pacific side, to Murmansk, on the European side. It does not go anywhere near the Persian Gulf. For Gulf oil to reach China via the Arctic, a tanker would need to leave the Gulf, sail either around Africa or through Suez into the Atlantic, cross the entire Atlantic north to the Arctic Ocean, traverse the length of Russia's Arctic coast eastward, pass back through the Bering Strait, and only then turn south to Chinese ports. That is not a shortcut. That is sailing around three sides of the planet to reach a destination that sits roughly ten to twelve days away by the direct route across the Indian Ocean.

No tanker does this, and none is going to. Which means the Ice Silk Road, however it develops over the coming years, cannot touch the flow that actually defines China's chokepoint vulnerability: energy. Roughly 80 percent of China's crude oil imports, worth some $312 billion annually according to an Observer Research Foundation analysis of US Energy Information Administration data, transit the Strait of Malacca. That flow originates in the Gulf and East Africa, moves across the Indian Ocean past the Andaman and Nicobar Islands, and threads the strait between Indonesia, Malaysia and Singapore. Every barrel of it remains exactly as exposed to the leverage India has held for two decades as it was before the Dubai Tower ever left port.

The Arctic route is, and structurally can only ever be, a Europe-facing corridor. Within that, it favors Northern and Western Europe specifically, Felixstowe, Rotterdam, Hamburg, the Baltic ports, where a ship exiting the Arctic can reach port with minimal additional sailing. Southern Europe barely benefits at all; a ship would need to round the entire European coastline after leaving the ice, eroding most of the time saved. The route says nothing about, and does nothing for, China's trade with the Middle East, Africa, Southeast Asia, the Americas or the rest of the Indo-Pacific, which together account for the overwhelming bulk of Chinese seaborne commerce.

Why Beijing is building it anyway

None of this means the Ice Silk Road is theater. It means it is a hedge, not an escape route, and the distinction matters for how seriously to take it.

The strategic logic traces back further than this year's headlines. China's anxiety over Malacca is not new; it dates to at least 2003, when Hu Jintao first articulated what Chinese strategists came to call the Malacca Dilemma, the fear that a hostile power could choke off China's trade at a single narrow point it does not control. Xi Jinping proposed the Polar Silk Road concept in 2017, and Beijing formalized it in a 2018 white paper that declared China a "near-Arctic state," a designation that raised eyebrows given China has no Arctic territory at all.

What has changed is the depth of the Russian partnership underwriting it. In October 2025, Rosatom's chief executive Alexei Likhachev and China's transport minister signed an agreement in Harbin setting a roadmap for 20 million tonnes of annual Russia-China cargo on the NSR by 2030, alongside joint shipbuilding, plans for five ice-class container ships, and crew training arrangements sending Chinese cadets to Russian maritime academies. In October 2024, China's Coast Guard entered the Arctic Ocean for the first time in its history, patrolling jointly with Russia's Border Guard near the Bering Strait, the closest Chinese vessels have come to Alaska under US Coast Guard observation. A newly released US congressional commission fact sheet counted at least 119 combined China-Russia military exercises as of April 2026, with joint naval operations in July involving submarines, anti-submarine assets and drones.

That is the part worth watching closely, more than any tonnage figure. Republican Congressman Michael Chang told Fox News the American response has been inadequate: "We need to be in the Arctic, and President Trump has authorized more icebreakers. We are woefully short in that department." The United States operates roughly three aging polar icebreakers against Russia's fleet of more than 40. One American official identified the one geographic constraint still working in Washington's favor: ships bound for the NSR "have to go between Russia on the west and our 49th state on the east," giving the US at least a monitoring position at the route's Pacific gateway.

India's own quiet Arctic move

The most striking new detail is not about China at all. It is about India.

According to reporting from The Economic Times and confirmed at the Arctic Regions Forum in Arkhangelsk, India is now looking to send its own first cargo vessel along the Northern Sea Route, targeted for 2027. Venkatesapathy S., Joint Secretary at India's Ministry of Ports, Shipping and Waterways, described India-Russia Arctic cooperation as moving "from dialogue to practical cooperation," framing it as an extension of the existing Chennai-Vladivostok Eastern Maritime Corridor. Archangelsk's regional governor, Alexander Tsybulsky, confirmed the 2027 timeline publicly. South Korea, separately, is preparing its own demonstration voyage on the same route.

This complicates any narrative that casts the Arctic purely as a China-versus-India story. New Delhi's interest is not about neutralizing Beijing's Malacca exposure; it is about diversifying India's own trade and energy corridors with Russia, and about not being the only major Indian Ocean power absent from a route that Japan, South Korea and China are all now actively testing. But it also underlines a gap that Indian strategic writing has flagged for several years: India holds Arctic Council observer status, alongside China, Japan, South Korea and Singapore, with no vote on how the region is governed, and its 2022 Arctic Policy document has no dedicated security or geopolitical pillar. India has no icebreaker of its own. A June 2025 memorandum of understanding with Norway's Kongsberg for an indigenous polar research vessel, and preliminary talks with Rosatom on non-nuclear icebreaker capability, are early steps, not present capacity.

An Amity University paper by Ishita Tripathi and S.S. Bindra, circulated in Indian strategic-studies circles, makes the underlying argument: that the US treats the Arctic primarily as a competition with Russia, and India treats it primarily as scientific engagement, and both miss how Beijing is building capability across the Arctic and the Indo-Pacific as a single, integrated maritime picture. Whatever one makes of the framing, the observer-status fact is not in dispute, and neither is the absence of an Indian icebreaker.

The verdict

None of this adds up to India's Malacca leverage being under any near-term threat. The arithmetic is too lopsided: a seasonal, Europe-only corridor carrying under a tenth of a percent of relevant trade cannot meaningfully offset an energy chokepoint carrying 80 percent of China's oil imports. Independent shipping analysis is blunt about the ceiling here; one Chinese academic study using GTAP trade modeling found the Arctic route saves China roughly 1 percent on overall shipping costs to Europe, not the transformative figure the "Golden Waterway" branding implies.

What has genuinely changed, and what deserves the sustained attention India's strategic community has not yet given it, is the trajectory of the Russia-China Arctic partnership itself: the joint shipbuilding, the coast guard patrols, the crew training, the 2030 tonnage targets, the military exercises running in parallel. None of that shows up in this year's eight container sailings. All of it is aimed at a decade further out, when a wider ice-free season and a larger ice-class fleet could plausibly turn a seasonal hedge into something closer to a standing corridor, though even the most rigorous peer-reviewed navigation modeling puts genuinely reliable year-round shipping for ordinary vessels closer to the 2060s than the 2030s.

The Dubai Tower is not, on its own, a threat to the strait India sits beside. What it represents, more precisely, is Beijing's admission that the strait still controls it, and Moscow's calculated decision to make sure that whatever alternative China eventually builds, it builds on Russian terms, through Russian waters, escorted by Russian ships.
