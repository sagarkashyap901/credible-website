CIA Chief Warned Putin Against Striking NATO. Now Officials Weigh a Nuclear Option.

A data-based account of the economic war behind the diplomatic one. Russia's refineries are running at a 24-year low and its budget deficit is set to double, even as global oil prices rise.

Sagar Kashyap · 28 Aug 2026

On 25 August 2026, a US Air Force C-17 landed at Moscow's Vnukovo airport carrying CIA Director John Ratcliffe. It was the first visit by a sitting CIA director to Russia since November 2021, when William Burns flew to Moscow to warn the Kremlin against invading Ukraine. That warning did not hold. The invasion followed roughly four months later.

The parallel is not coincidental framing. It is the reason the trip is being read as seriously as it is. But understanding why Washington sent its intelligence chief to Moscow this time requires starting somewhere other than the trip itself: with a number.

The number that explains everything

Russia's oil and gas revenue in the first half of 2026 came to 64 percent of what it was two years earlier, according to figures cited by the Atlantic Council's GeoEconomics Center. Charles Lichfield, the center's director of economic foresight and analysis, told CNBC that Russia is on course to double the budget deficit it ran in 2025 — a deficit that had already doubled the one recorded in 2024.

This should not be happening. Global oil prices have been elevated for months, driven by the closure of the Strait of Hormuz during the parallel US-Iran war. High prices are supposed to be exactly the condition under which a petrostate's finances improve. Instead, Russia is recording its worst fiscal year of the war.

The reason is not sanctions in the traditional sense. It is drones.

The refinery campaign: from selective strikes to total coverage

Ukraine's long-range strike campaign against Russian oil infrastructure did not begin in 2026, attacks trace back to 2024, but it reached a different order of magnitude this year. The operation Ukrainian officials called "40 Days of Pressure" ran through 4 August 2026 and covered roughly 100 strategic targets. Its centerpiece was the 7 July strike on the Omsk refinery, Russia's single largest, located more than 2,500 kilometers from the Ukrainian border, the longest-range confirmed drone strike in history. With Omsk hit, Ukraine had now struck all eleven of Russia's largest refineries at least once.

The measured impact, independent of Ukrainian claims, is stark. Bloomberg, citing EA Analytics data, reported that Russian refineries processed an estimated 3.6 million barrels of crude per day in July 2026, the lowest level since May 2002, and roughly a third below the seasonal norm for the years 2020 through 2025, when average throughput ran between 5.3 and 5.6 million barrels per day. Russian Forbes separately reported that 54 percent of the country's oil refineries had sustained damage. Domestic petrol output fell to between 75,000 and 80,000 tonnes a day against summer demand of 115,000 to 120,000 tonnes, a supply deficit of roughly 35 percent that produced visible fuel shortages and rationing across Russian regions.

The campaign continued past its formal end date. Ukraine's General Staff confirmed a 10 August strike on the Zapsibneftekhim petrochemical facility in Tobolsk damaged its central gas fractionation unit. An 11 August strike on the Orsknefteorgsintez refinery in Orenburg hit a hydrocracker unit, three primary processing units, and a secondary distillation unit. On the night of 15–16 August, Ukrainian forces struck the Kamensky Plant in Rostov Oblast, which the General Staff identified as a producer of solid rocket fuel for Russian multiple-launch rocket systems and missiles, a deliberate shift from pure energy infrastructure toward the defense-industrial base that depends on it.

Export revenue: the numbers behind the collapse

The Centre for Research on Energy and Clean Air (CREA), which publishes monthly tracking of Russian fossil fuel exports, recorded a 12 percent month-on-month fall in total fossil fuel export revenue in July 2026, to €683 million per day. The composition of that decline is instructive. Pipeline crude export earnings fell 21 percent month-on-month, partially offset by a 7 percent rise in seaborne crude revenue as Russia redirected volume toward tankers. But refined product exports, the higher-margin output that refineries exist to produce, collapsed. Russian oil product export loadings fell 23 percent month-on-month to 4.7 million tonnes in July, the lowest level on record and less than half the 9.6 million tonnes loaded in the same month one year earlier. LNG export revenue fell 36 percent month-on-month over the same period.

This is the mechanism by which drone strikes translate into fiscal crisis. A refinery that cannot process crude cannot produce the diesel, aviation fuel, and other refined products that carry the highest export margins. Crude can still be pumped and sold at a discount; the value-added step is what breaks.

Two structural facts widen the gap further. Heavy fuel subsidies that the Russian government pays to domestic oil companies, designed to prevent international price spikes from reaching Russian consumers directly, absorb a large share of whatever revenue does arrive, even in a high-price environment. And 53 percent of Russia's seaborne oil in July 2026 moved on sanctioned "shadow fleet" tankers, a logistics structure that carries its own cost and risk premium built into every barrel shipped.

Wildberries: the second front

If refineries were the obvious target, Ukraine's second major 2026 campaign was not. Beginning 18 July, Ukrainian drones began striking warehouses belonging to Wildberries, Russia's largest e-commerce platform and a company that accounts for roughly 10 percent of the country's entire retail market.

The scale, documented independently, is significant. Reuters reported in early August that strikes had destroyed at least 1.18 million square meters of Wildberries warehouse space, one-fifth of the company's total capacity. By 16 August, according to the Institute for the Study of War, Ukrainian forces had struck and halted operations at seven of the company's ten largest logistics centers, including its single biggest hub: a 250,000-square-meter facility in the Koledino Industrial Park near Podolsk, roughly 420 kilometers inside Russian territory. Foreign Policy estimated that by 22 July, after only the campaign's opening days, physical damage to the warehouses alone stood at roughly $400 million, with an additional $2 billion in seller inventory losses that sellers themselves have little prospect of recovering.

The strategic logic, according to Ukrainian officials, rests on two claims. First, that Wildberries facilities have been used to supply the Russian military with dual-use goods, including drone components and navigation equipment, an allegation Wildberries and the Kremlin both deny. Second, and confirmed more explicitly by Ukrainian presidential adviser Mykhailo Podolyak in a subsequent interview, that the deeper objective is macroeconomic: degrading a company whose scale gives it systemic weight in Russia's banking sector. Fire Point co-founder Denys Shtilerman argued publicly that Wildberries' financing relationship with state-controlled VTB Bank, as, by his account, Russia's largest single corporate borrower, makes its failure a mechanism for transmitting damage directly into the banking system.

The strikes reached St. Petersburg on 24 July, a city that had been largely spared throughout the war given the concentration of air defenses protecting Putin's home city. Two logistics hubs were hit, the Shushary facility and the Utkina Zavod complex, producing smoke plumes visible in NASA satellite imagery. Wildberries co-founder Tatyana Kim, reported to be Russia's wealthiest woman, confirmed operations had been suspended at both sites.

The ISW's assessment of the campaign's significance goes beyond the immediate economic damage: Russia's inability to protect Wildberries' largest hubs, none of which are near the front line, demonstrates that the country lacks sufficient air defense capacity to simultaneously cover the war zone and its own economic interior. Every asset committed to defending Moscow-region logistics parks is an asset unavailable elsewhere.

A secondary effect has emerged industry-wide. Because Wildberries' scale made centralized, high-efficiency warehousing the retail sector norm, competing logistics firms have begun dispersing their own operations to reduce vulnerability to the same tactic, a shift that analysts expect to reduce sector-wide logistics efficiency even for companies Ukraine never targets directly.

What Ratcliffe was reportedly there to say

Against this economic backdrop, the substance of the Ratcliffe visit becomes legible. The Wall Street Journal reported that US intelligence assessments, described by officials as reflecting a genuine shift from the prior consensus that Putin would avoid provoking NATO while still engaged in Ukraine, now consider it plausible that Putin could attempt to test NATO's resolve with a limited action against a member state. Scenarios described in the reporting range from cyberattacks to the deployment of unmarked armed groups to occupy contested territory to a small-scale ground incursion, with one source specifically naming the Baltic states or Poland as the most probable locations. The assessed window for such action runs from autumn 2026 through 2029, and the probability of the most severe scenario, a limited ground incursion, is characterized in the reporting as low but rising over time.

Washington's own capacity to respond is constrained by a second, simultaneous conflict. American munitions stockpiles, including long-range Precision Strike Missiles, Army Tactical Missile Systems, and Stinger air-defense missiles, have been depleted by continued transfers to Ukraine and by the US war against Iran, which began on 28 February 2026. Heather Conley of the American Enterprise Institute noted that undermining NATO's credibility and driving a wedge between the United States and its allies has "always been" a Russian strategic objective; the open question, in her framing, is how Washington would actually respond if tested.

Corroborating evidence of an active hybrid campaign against NATO infrastructure preceded the CIA visit by three weeks. On 4 August, an explosive-laden drone struck a Ukrainian cargo plane parked at Leipzig/Halle Airport in Germany, a facility used by NATO for its Strategic Airlift International Solution mission delivering equipment to Ukraine and eastern allies. US officials assessed the drone belonged to a Russian intelligence service and was carrying military-grade explosives. German Interior Minister Alexander Dobrindt described the incident as marking "a new level of danger," distinguishing it from the surveillance-oriented drone incidents, 18 of which had disrupted air traffic at that airport alone since January 2026, that had characterized the pattern previously.

Prior to the Ratcliffe visit, Washington asked Kyiv to suspend strikes on Russian territory for its duration, a request with no obvious rationale under a "routine" characterization of the trip. The Kremlin confirmed Ratcliffe met Russian intelligence officials during an hours-long visit but did not meet Putin directly, who was reportedly briefed on the discussions afterward. President Trump subsequently told Glenn Beck the trip was "semi-routine" and was not intended to warn Putin against attacking NATO territory, a public account that stands in direct tension with the reported substance of the visit as described by intelligence sources.

The Bloomberg report: escalation as the only remaining option

One day after Ratcliffe's visit, on 26 August 2026, Bloomberg published a report, sourced to three people described as close to the Kremlin, that supplies the clearest picture yet of Moscow's internal reasoning. The central finding: Russia has concluded that negotiations have reached a dead end, and is actively weighing an intensification of conventional ballistic missile attacks on Kyiv, including strikes on the city center, alongside attacks on infrastructure targets in other Ukrainian cities.

The sourcing lays out a specific internal logic, and it inverts the assumption that economic pressure produces capitulation. According to Bloomberg's sources, Putin will not end the war under pressure from sanctions or from Ukrainian strikes on Russian oil refineries and logistics networks. Moscow is increasingly reclassifying Ukrainian deep strikes rhetorically as attacks by NATO member states, on the reasoning that alliance countries supply Kyiv with the weapons and intelligence that make the strikes possible, a framing that lays rhetorical groundwork for retaliation against NATO-linked targets. Every formal mechanism for negotiation has collapsed. And the Kremlin's internal assessment holds that each concession Russia made earlier in the war left it in a weaker position than before, leaving escalation as the path of least resistance in the absence of any functioning diplomatic track.

Kremlin officials retain only slim hopes that US envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner might bring new proposals in a subsequent visit; Bloomberg's sourcing describes expectations of a breakthrough as "exceptionally low."

The nuclear question, reported precisely

Buried within the same Bloomberg report is the passage that has generated the most public attention, and it deserves to be reproduced with its qualifications intact rather than compressed.

The intensifying pace of Ukrainian strikes has led a growing number of Russian officials to believe that Putin could eventually take the extreme step of using tactical nuclear weapons in Ukraine. Bloomberg's own reporting states plainly, in the same passage, that there are no signs Russia is currently preparing to do so. The outlet noted that Russia has a documented history of making nuclear threats specifically to intimidate Ukraine's Western allies, without following through on them.

The actual risk of Moscow using a tactical nuclear weapon "remains extremely low."

That assessment comes from Alexander Gabuev, director of the Berlin-based Carnegie Russia Eurasia Center, speaking to Bloomberg. Separate reporting on the same Bloomberg story added that Russia's international partners, including China, strongly oppose any such move and have communicated that opposition directly to the Kremlin.

The distinction being reported is specific and should not be collapsed in the retelling: this is a claim about the internal composition of opinion inside Russian officialdom, a rising number of officials treating nuclear use as a theoretically available last resort, not a claim about military preparation, doctrine change, or operational planning. Bloomberg's own framing draws that line explicitly. Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov, responding to the broader Bloomberg report, said Russia does not need further military escalation because its forces are already advancing on the ground, a claim that itself sits in tension with battlefield data, discussed below, showing no confirmed territorial movement on either side through the second half of August.

The battlefield context Moscow is not advertising

The economic and hybrid-warfare pressure detailed above is occurring alongside, not instead of, a grinding and largely static front line. The Institute for the Study of War's assessment for 16 August 2026 recorded that neither Russian nor Ukrainian forces made confirmed advances anywhere along the front that day, with Russian offensive operations continuing without territorial gain across Sumy, northern Kharkiv, the Kupyansk and Borova directions, Slovyansk, Dobropillya, Pokrovsk, Novopavlivka, Hulyaipole, and western Zaporizhzhia Oblast.

Russian forces have instead shifted toward a battlefield air interdiction campaign against Ukraine's Donetsk "fortress belt," striking Slovyansk, Kramatorsk, and Druzhkivka with glide bombs and drones to degrade ground lines of communication ahead of a prospective future offensive, rather than mounting one now. ISW separately assessed that Ukrainian forces liberated at least 626 square kilometers of tactically significant territory in the Oleksandrivka direction between January and August 2026.

This picture should not be read as uniformly favorable to Ukraine. Ukraine's own air force stopped publishing figures on Russian missiles launched and intercepted as of 11 August 2026, a change Bloomberg reported reflects strain on Ukrainian air defenses rather than improved conditions. In the single week of 9–15 August, Russian forces struck 13 Ukrainian regions with more than 1,550 strike drones, nearly 1,560 glide bombs, and 62 missiles, the large majority ballistic. A Ukrainian deputy battalion commander operating in the Sumy direction told the Ukrainian outlet Suspilne on 16 August that Russian forces continue conducting effective counterbattery fire and "show no signs of any weapons shortages" at the tactical level, even as the national economic picture deteriorates.

The manpower dimension

A third pressure point compounds the fiscal and infrastructural ones: Russia's contract-based recruitment system, which has funded force replenishment without a formal mobilization since 2022, is reportedly no longer keeping pace with battlefield losses. President Zelensky said on 23 August, citing Ukrainian intelligence, that Russia is preparing to mobilize an additional 300,000 personnel following the State Duma elections scheduled for 18–21 September 2026, with the possibility of a further tranche reaching a cumulative total of 500,000 by 2027. Zelensky characterized the plan as a deliberately "peripheral" mobilization, concentrated in Russia's remote regions rather than Moscow or St. Petersburg, timed to begin in early October, after the electoral calendar has closed rather than before it, to minimize domestic political exposure.

Russian officials have publicly disputed the claim. Deputy Security Council Chairman Dmitry Medvedev called the reports "nonsense" and a Ukrainian "provocation," citing approximately 200,000 contract enlistments recorded in the first half of 2026 as evidence that further mobilization is unnecessary. Russia's UN ambassador, Vasily Nebenzya, said on 20 August that mass mobilization is "not planned or expected" as far as he is aware, a formulation that notably stops short of a categorical denial.

Reading the sequence

Laid end to end, the sequence of the past six weeks supports a specific and falsifiable interpretation, distinct from the more alarmist framings that have circulated since the Ratcliffe trip became public.

Ukraine's dual campaign against Russian refining capacity and Wildberries' logistics network has produced measurable, independently verified fiscal damage, a doubling deficit trajectory, a 24-year low in refining output, and export revenue collapsing even as global oil prices rise. That economic pressure has not, according to the Kremlin's own described internal reasoning as relayed to Bloomberg, produced any movement toward negotiated settlement. Instead, with every diplomatic channel assessed as exhausted, it has narrowed the Kremlin's perceived options to further military escalation, first against Ukraine directly, and second, in a lower-probability but rising-over-time scenario now taken seriously enough to warrant a CIA director's personal visit, against NATO territory itself. The nuclear dimension exists within this picture specifically as a shift in internal Russian official sentiment under sustained pressure, explicitly not as a reported change in military posture or preparation.

The strategic conclusion is a paradox rather than a straight line: an economic pressure campaign succeeding in its immediate objective, making the war progressively less affordable for Russia to sustain, is simultaneously removing the conditions under which that success would normally be expected to produce peace.
