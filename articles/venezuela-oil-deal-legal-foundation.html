Washington and Caracas Announced the Same Oil Deal. They Described Two Different Agreements.

US officials say a new venture holds a 100-year concession over 17 Venezuelan oil fields, with Washington taking 55 percent of output. Venezuela's interim president says the project runs 25 years and that her country retains sovereignty over its resources. Neither government has released the contract.

Sagar Kashyap · 29 Aug 2026 · Updated 30 Aug 2026

A note on sourcing

This analysis separates what is verified from what is asserted. On this story, that distinction is doing unusually heavy work.

Confirmed: President Trump's announcement on August 28. Interim President Delcy Rodríguez's televised response on August 29. The involvement of the State Department and the Department of War in negotiations. The January 29, 2026 amendment to Venezuela's Organic Hydrocarbons Law, published in Special Official Gazette No. 6.978.

Reported: The 55 percent effective output share, split between an equity stake and an at-cost offtake right. The 17 fields. The 65 billion barrels of proven reserves. Attributed to unnamed US officials briefing CBS News, Newsweek, ABC News, The Hill and the Associated Press.

Disputed: The duration of the agreement. US officials describe a 100-year concession. Rodríguez, on state television, described a 25-year bilateral project. Also disputed is the fundamental characterization, with Trump claiming majority US control while Rodríguez states that Venezuela retains ownership and sovereignty over its resources.

Claimed: The nearly $100 billion in private investment. The $209 billion in projected tax revenue for Caracas. The production target above 1.5 million barrels per day. The assertion that the deal will lower American pump prices.

Inferred: The identity of the private operator, which neither government has disclosed. The legal structure under which the venture is incorporated. The timeframe over which the revenue projections apply.

No contract text has been published by either side.

What was actually announced

The arrangement covers 17 Venezuelan oil fields containing an estimated 65 billion barrels of proven reserves. Those barrels sit inside a newly created venture, described by a US official as a joint project of the American government and an experienced private operator in Venezuela.

The United States takes 55 percent of that venture. Officials have said the figure combines two distinct rights: an equity stake in the company, and a contractual right to purchase oil at production cost rather than market price. The remainder stays with the Venezuelan side and the unnamed operator.

That distinction matters, and it is where most coverage has gone wrong. The United States did not acquire 65 billion barrels. It acquired a claim on roughly 36 billion barrels of eventual output, realized over time as oil is extracted. On OPEC's reserve accounting, that moves the American effective position from 45 billion barrels to roughly 81 billion, from tenth place to eighth, narrowly ahead of Russia.

The venture would rank as the world's second largest corporate holder of proven reserves after Saudi Aramco.

The duration problem

On Friday night, US officials told at least four American news organizations that Rodríguez had granted the venture 100-year concessions over the fields.

On Saturday, Rodríguez went on VTV and described something different. She called it a 25-year bilateral project covering 17 strategic oilfields with a production target above 1.5 million barrels per day. She added that Venezuela retains ownership and sovereignty over its resources, and framed the arrangement as each side contributing what it does best: Venezuela the oil and the workforce, the United States the capital and technology.

Two governments, one agreement, two durations, announced within 24 hours of each other.

There are three plausible explanations, and they carry very different implications.

They are describing different instruments. A 100-year concession grant could contain a 25-year development and production commitment inside it. This is the most charitable reading and the most likely one. But it would mean US officials briefed the longer figure while Venezuela's head of state briefed the shorter one, each choosing the number that suited their audience. That is a presentation problem, not a legal one, though it does not inspire confidence in either account.

They are describing different agreements. Rodríguez was explicit that her 1.5 million barrel figure related solely to the bilateral agreement between Venezuela and the United States. That phrasing implies the existence of a broader arrangement she was distinguishing it from. If the venture's concession and the bilateral agreement are separate legal objects, the public has been given fragments of two documents and told they are one deal.

One side is overstating. The Trump administration has a domestic political interest in a permanent-sounding acquisition ahead of midterms with gasoline near $4.09 a gallon. Rodríguez has an acute domestic political interest in not appearing to have signed away her country's patrimony for a century. Both incentives point exactly where the discrepancy sits.

Why the duration determines everything else

The gap between 25 and 100 years is not a detail. It changes the legal character of the agreement, its value, and its survivability.

On legality. Venezuela abolished its concession system in the 1975 nationalization law, which ended existing concessions and reserved the industry to the state. Pre-nationalization Venezuelan concessions ran 40 years. A 100-year grant would exceed anything Venezuelan law has permitted since 1943 and would require specific statutory authority that has not been publicly identified in the January reform. A 25-year term, by contrast, sits comfortably within international norms. Argentine concessions run 25 years with renewal options. A 25-year production sharing arrangement would be an ordinary commercial instrument requiring no exotic legal reasoning.

Put plainly: if the term is 25 years, the deal is unusual in scale but conventional in form. If it is 100 years, it is a legal instrument Venezuela has not recognized in fifty years.

On value. Extracting 65 billion barrels of Orinoco heavy crude is a multi-decade undertaking. A 25-year window at 1.5 million barrels per day yields roughly 13.7 billion barrels, about a fifth of the stated reserves. A century-long grant captures nearly all of it. The two scenarios describe assets that differ in value by several multiples.

On durability. A 25-year agreement expires within one political generation and can be renegotiated at term rather than repudiated. A 100-year grant issued by an interim government invites challenge precisely because no successor can wait it out.

The structural question nobody has answered

Venezuela's 1999 Constitution establishes in Article 12 that hydrocarbon deposits belong to the Republic and remain in the public domain. Article 303 requires the state to retain all shares of PDVSA, with a carve-out for subsidiaries, strategic associations and joint ventures that Venezuelan constitutional scholars have long identified as the opening for private participation.

The January 29 reform, approved unanimously by the National Assembly and signed the same day by Rodríguez, is the most significant change to Venezuela's oil regime since 1975. It repealed the 2006 law reserving upstream activity to the Republic, the 2009 law reserving hydrocarbon assets and services, the 2013 windfall tax, and Decree 5,200 governing the Orinoco Belt migration.

The reform establishes three permissible structures for primary activities: directly by the Executive or by companies wholly owned by the Republic; by joint ventures in which the Republic holds a majority stake conferring shareholder control; or by private companies domiciled in Venezuela operating under contracts with state-owned entities.

It also grants minority shareholders in mixed companies rights they previously lacked: authority to directly market production, to hold accounts in any currency or jurisdiction, and to exercise technical and operational management. Royalties, previously fixed at 30 percent, may be reduced to 20 percent for private company contracts and 15 percent for joint ventures where a project is demonstrably uneconomic at the standard rate.

US officials have repeatedly called this arrangement a joint venture. But under the amended law, a joint venture requires Venezuelan state majority control. A structure giving the United States 55 percent cannot be a joint venture as the statute defines one.

It could only be lawful as the third category, a private company operating under contract with a state entity, where no majority requirement applies. So the two governments disagree about the duration, and the American description of the structure does not match what Venezuelan law permits for a structure by that name. Both problems resolve the moment either side publishes the contract. Neither has.

Can a future Venezuelan government break it?

This is the central risk for American capital, and the duration dispute makes it sharper rather than easier to assess.

Legitimacy of the grantor. An agreement signed by an interim government installed after a foreign military operation carries a specific vulnerability. Any successor has both a legal argument, that the signing authority lacked democratic mandate, and a powerful domestic incentive to make it. Venezuela's 2007 nationalizations under Hugo Chávez established both the precedent and the playbook. Rodríguez's insistence that Venezuela retains sovereignty reads, in this light, as pre-emptive defense against exactly that charge.

The term itself. A 25-year agreement is defensible domestically as a commercial arrangement with a defined end. A 100-year concession is the single most politically combustible form the deal could take in Venezuelan politics, and the easiest for a nationalist successor to campaign against. Which number is real substantially determines how much annulment pressure the agreement will face.

Arbitration exposure. The reform preserved international arbitration and mechanisms for maintaining the economic and financial balance of agreements. If the venture is covered by an arbitration clause, unilateral annulment becomes expensive rather than impossible. Venezuela has an extensive record of adverse ICSID awards, most of them unpaid, which is a warning about the practical value of winning such a case.

Creditor attachment. Venezuela and PDVSA carry billions in unpaid arbitration awards and defaulted bonds. A newly valuable asset is a newly attractive target. Twelve House Democrats led by Representative Sean Casten warned 21 oil and oilfield services companies in January that arrangements resting on asserted executive authority over Venezuelan state assets could be invalidated by Congress, by a future administration, or by a future Venezuelan government, and could expose participating firms to creditor claims.

Enforcement asymmetry. The practical constraint is political rather than legal. A successor government weighing annulment must weigh the response of a United States that has already demonstrated willingness to remove a Venezuelan head of state. That is not a legal defense. It is the operative one.

What it means for India

India built a Venezuelan supply relationship at speed this year. Reliance resumed direct purchases from PDVSA in April under a US Treasury licence, receiving Boscan crude at Sikka for the first time in a year. Volumes reached roughly 283,000 barrels per day in April and an estimated 380,000 by June. Latin American crude rose from 3.5 percent to 12.7 percent of India's import basket between April and July, while Middle East supply fell from about 43 percent to 30 percent.

That was a Hormuz hedge, and it worked.

The risk is not that Venezuelan oil becomes unavailable to India. Nearly $100 billion of promised investment into capital-starved fields should raise output over time, and heavy sour crude suits Jamnagar's configuration well.

The risk is that access now runs through Washington.

Every barrel India buys from Venezuela flows under an OFAC licence that can be modified or revoked. ONGC Videsh, which holds stakes in San Cristóbal and Carabobo-1, has been awaiting its own licence under the new regime. India spent five months substituting a physical chokepoint it cannot control for a regulatory chokepoint it also cannot control. Hormuz reopens with a ceasefire. A licence is revoked with a signature.

There is a commercial dimension as well. India was buying Venezuelan crude at a sanctions discount, and that discount is what made the trade attractive. Normalization erodes it. The US Gulf Coast, with the world's densest concentration of heavy sour refining capacity and a short Caribbean haul, is the geographically natural buyer for these barrels. India competes from farther away, against a buyer holding an at-cost purchase right.

The duration dispute matters here too. A 25-year project targeting 1.5 million barrels per day implies a specific export volume that Indian refiners can plan against. A century-long concession with no stated output commitment does not. Until the term is clarified, Indian buyers are underwriting supply agreements against an asset whose basic parameters remain publicly contested.

What to watch

Whether either government publishes contract text, and whether the 25-year and 100-year figures describe the same instrument. Whether the private operator is identified, and whether it is American, Venezuelan or third-country. Which of the three statutory structures the venture uses, and whether it is legally a joint venture at all. Whether ONGC Videsh's pending licence is granted, delayed or quietly shelved. Whether Venezuelan opposition figures, exiled jurists or PDVSA creditors file formal challenges. And SPR refill volumes over the next four quarters, the clearest measure of whether the at-cost offtake right is being exercised.
