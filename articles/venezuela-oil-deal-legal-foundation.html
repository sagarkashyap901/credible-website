US Claims Majority Control Over Venezuela's Oil. Can the Deal Legally Hold Up?

The Trump administration announced majority control over output from 17 Venezuelan oil fields holding 65 billion barrels. The arrangement moves the United States past Russia in effective reserves without a single new well drilled on American soil. It also rests on a structure that Venezuela's own amended hydrocarbons law may not permit, granted by an interim government installed after American forces removed the country's president.

Sagar Kashyap · 29 Aug 2026

A note on sourcing

This analysis separates what is verified from what is asserted.

Confirmed: The announcement itself, made by President Trump on August 28. The involvement of the State Department and the Department of War. The January 29, 2026 amendment to Venezuela's Organic Hydrocarbons Law and its contents, published in Special Official Gazette No. 6.978.

Reported: The 55 percent effective output share, split between an equity stake and an at-cost offtake right. The 17 fields. The 65 billion barrels. The 100-year concession term. All sourced to US officials briefing CNN, NPR and the Associated Press.

Claimed: The nearly $100 billion in private investment cited by Secretary of State Marco Rubio. The $209 billion projected flow to Venezuela's treasury. The assertion that the deal will lower American pump prices.

Inferred: The identity of the private operator, which has not been disclosed. The timeframe over which the revenue projections apply. The legal structure under which the venture is incorporated.

No contract text has been published.

What was actually signed

The arrangement covers 17 Venezuelan oil fields containing an estimated 65 billion barrels of proven reserves. Those barrels sit inside a newly created venture, not in American hands directly.

The United States takes 55 percent of that venture's output. Officials describing the terms have said this figure combines two distinct rights: an equity stake in the company, and a contractual right to purchase oil at production cost rather than market price. The remaining 45 percent stays with the Venezuelan side and an unnamed private operator.

That distinction matters, and it is where most coverage has gone wrong. The United States did not acquire 65 billion barrels. It acquired a claim on roughly 36 billion barrels of eventual output, exercised over time as oil is extracted.

The concession term is reported at 100 years. It was granted by interim President Delcy Rodríguez, who assumed office after US forces captured Nicolás Maduro in January and transported him to the United States to face narcoterrorism charges.

How America benefits

The reserve arithmetic. By OPEC's Annual Statistical Bulletin, the United States holds 45 billion barrels of proven reserves, ranking tenth globally, behind Libya and Russia. Adding a 55 percent claim on the new venture brings the effective figure to roughly 81 billion barrels. That moves the United States to eighth place, narrowly ahead of Russia's 80 billion.

The strategic reserve. The Strategic Petroleum Reserve fell below 300 million barrels in early August, down more than 100 million from prior levels. Officials have said the at-cost offtake is earmarked for SPR refill and military fuel requirements. This is the most operationally concrete benefit in the deal, and the least discussed.

Hemispheric supply security. Persian Gulf crude exports remain roughly seven to eight million barrels per day below pre-war volumes, according to Goldman Sachs estimates, with Hormuz flows only partially restored. A Western Hemisphere heavy crude source under American commercial control reduces exposure to a chokepoint the United States cannot unilaterally reopen.

Domestic politics. US gasoline prices sat near $4.09 a gallon in late August, up 27 percent year over year. Midterm elections follow in November.

The gasoline claim, however, does not survive scrutiny. Orinoco Belt crude is heavy and high in sulfur, requiring substantial upgrading capacity and years of capital investment before meaningful volumes reach market. Nothing in this arrangement affects pump prices in the current election cycle.

The legal foundation, and why it is contested

Venezuela's 1999 Constitution establishes in Article 12 that hydrocarbon deposits belong to the Republic and remain in the public domain. Article 303 requires the state to retain ownership of all shares of PDVSA, with an explicit carve-out for subsidiaries, strategic associations and joint ventures. That carve-out has long been described by Venezuelan constitutional scholars as a structural opening for private participation.

The January 29 reform, approved unanimously by the National Assembly and signed the same day by Rodríguez, is the most significant change to Venezuela's oil regime since nationalization in 1975. It repealed the 2006 law reserving upstream activity to the Republic, the 2009 law reserving hydrocarbon assets and services, the 2013 windfall tax, and Decree 5,200 governing the Orinoco Belt migration.

Critically, the reform establishes three permissible structures for primary activities: directly by the Executive or by companies wholly owned by the Republic; by joint ventures in which the Republic holds a majority stake conferring shareholder control; or by private companies domiciled in Venezuela operating under contracts with state-owned entities.

The reform also grants minority shareholders in mixed companies rights they did not previously hold: authority to directly market production, to open and manage accounts in any currency or jurisdiction, and to exercise technical and operational management of the venture.

Royalties, previously fixed at 30 percent, may now be reduced to 20 percent for private company contracts and 15 percent for joint ventures where a project is demonstrably uneconomic at the standard rate.

White House officials have described this arrangement as a joint venture. But under the amended law, a joint venture requires Venezuelan state majority control. A structure granting the United States 55 percent of output cannot be a joint venture as the statute defines one.

It can only be lawful as the third category, a private company operating under contract with a state entity, where no majority requirement applies. Which structure was used has not been disclosed. Until the contract text is published, whether this deal complies with Venezuela's own law remains an open question rather than a settled fact.

A second anomaly deserves attention. The 1975 nationalization law specifically ended Venezuela's concession system and allowed existing concessions to expire. The reported 100-year concession revives terminology that Venezuelan law abolished half a century ago. Whether the reform reintroduced concessions as a legal instrument, or whether the term is being used loosely to describe a long-term contract, has not been clarified.

Can a future Venezuelan government break it?

This is the central risk, and the honest answer is that it depends on three things.

Legitimacy of the grantor. An agreement signed by an interim government installed after a foreign military operation carries a specific vulnerability. Any successor government has both a legal argument, that the signing authority lacked democratic mandate, and a powerful domestic political incentive to make it. Venezuela's 2007 nationalizations under Hugo Chávez established the precedent and the playbook.

The arbitration question. The reform preserved international arbitration and mechanisms for maintaining the economic and financial balance of agreements. If the venture is protected by an arbitration clause and a bilateral investment treaty, unilateral annulment becomes expensive rather than impossible. Venezuela has been through this before and has an extensive record of adverse ICSID awards, most of them unpaid.

Enforcement asymmetry. The practical constraint is not legal but political. A successor government weighing annulment must weigh the consequences from a United States that has already demonstrated willingness to remove a Venezuelan head of state. That calculation is not a legal defense, but it is the real one.

Twelve House Democrats led by Representative Sean Casten anticipated much of this in January, writing to 21 oil and oilfield services companies to warn that arrangements resting on asserted executive authority over Venezuelan state assets could be invalidated by Congress, by a future administration, or by a future Venezuelan government, and could expose participating firms to claims from Venezuela's substantial creditor queue.

That queue is real. Venezuela and PDVSA carry billions in unpaid arbitration awards and defaulted bonds. A newly valuable asset is a newly attractive target for attachment.

What it means for India

India built a Venezuelan supply relationship at speed this year. Reliance resumed direct purchases from PDVSA in April under a US Treasury licence, receiving Boscan crude at Sikka for the first time in a year. Volumes reached roughly 283,000 barrels per day in April and an estimated 380,000 by June. Latin American crude rose from 3.5 percent to 12.7 percent of India's import basket between April and July, while Middle East supply fell from 43 percent to about 30 percent.

That was a Hormuz hedge, and it worked.

The risk is not that Venezuelan oil becomes unavailable to India. Nearly $100 billion of promised investment into capital-starved fields should raise output over time, and more heavy sour crude suits Jamnagar's configuration well.

The risk is that access now runs through Washington.

Every barrel India buys from Venezuela flows under an OFAC licence that can be modified or revoked. ONGC Videsh, which holds stakes in San Cristóbal and Carabobo-1, has been awaiting its own licence under the new regime. India spent five months substituting a physical chokepoint it cannot control for a regulatory chokepoint it also cannot control. Hormuz reopens with a ceasefire. A licence is revoked with a signature.

There is a commercial dimension as well. India was buying Venezuelan crude at a sanctions discount, and that discount is what made the trade attractive. Normalization erodes it. The US Gulf Coast, with the world's densest concentration of heavy sour refining capacity and a short Caribbean haul, is the geographically natural buyer for these barrels. India competes from farther away, against a buyer holding an at-cost purchase right.

What to watch

Whether the identity of the private operator is disclosed, and whether it is an American, Venezuelan or third-country entity. Whether any contract text is published, and which of the three statutory structures the venture uses. Whether ONGC Videsh's pending licence is granted, delayed or quietly shelved. Whether Venezuelan opposition figures, exiled jurists or PDVSA creditors file formal challenges. And SPR refill volumes over the next four quarters, the clearest measure of whether the at-cost offtake right is being exercised.
