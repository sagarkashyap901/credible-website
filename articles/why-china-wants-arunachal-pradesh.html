Why China Really Wants Arunachal Pradesh

Inside Beijing's six-decade campaign to contest an Indian state it has never governed.

Sagar Kashyap · 14 Aug 2026

Arunachal Pradesh is, has been, and will always be an integral and inalienable part of India. That has been New Delhi's unchanging position through six decades of Chinese pressure, and it is the frame within which everything below should be read. What follows is not a survey of competing claims. It is an examination of why Beijing keeps contesting a settled fact, what it has done about it over the past nine years, and what it is actually after.

The immediate flashpoint

On August 7, 2026, India's Ministry of Home Affairs formally identified 27 places and geographical features in Arunachal Pradesh by their standard names on official Survey of India maps. The ministry said the exercise was carried out in consultation with the Arunachal Pradesh state government and was intended to ensure "accurate recognition" of these locations and improve public awareness.

The list is deliberately loaded with sites that carry historical and strategic weight. It includes 21 land areas, four mountain passes, one lake and one war memorial. Among them: Longju, a village along the Line of Actual Control that has been under Chinese control since Chinese forces entered it in 1959, one of the earliest flashpoints in the entire border dispute, though it remains within India's claimed boundary. Also on the list are Thag La, the pass where one of the opening engagements of the 1962 war took place; Jaswant Garh, which houses the memorial to Jaswant Singh Rawat on the Tawang road; the Sher-e-Thapa Memorial commemorating 1962 war hero Trilok Singh Thapa; and Jairampur in Changlang district, a logistics hub supporting troop movement toward the Myanmar frontier. The four passes named are Dzo La, Riza La, Pukur La and Thag La.

This is not India's first rejection of Chinese naming claims. It is India's first attempt to put its own names permanently on the record instead of simply objecting to Beijing's.

China responded within three days. On August 10, its Foreign Ministry called India's move "illegal and invalid" and said it "cannot change the fact that Zangnan belongs to China," using Beijing's term for the state, meaning "South Tibet." India's Ministry of External Affairs rejected the characterization on August 11, with spokesperson Randhir Jaiswal saying Arunachal Pradesh's status as Indian territory is "self-evident" and that "nothing can change this indisputable reality." On August 12, a Chinese Foreign Ministry spokesperson, responding to separate questions about reported increases in People's Liberation Army activity near Upper Subansiri district, described the border situation as "generally stable" while pointing to an August 6 border-mechanism meeting as evidence of continued communication between the two sides, without directly addressing the specific activity reports.

A campaign, not an incident

China's naming exercise did not begin in 2026. It is the sixth in a sequence stretching back nine years, and the pattern is worth laying out in full because it shows this is systematic policy, not a one-off provocation.

April 2017: China's Ministry of Civil Affairs issued standardized Chinese, Tibetan and pinyin names for six locations in Arunachal Pradesh. The timing followed the Dalai Lama's visit to Tawang monastery that same month.

December 2021: A second list added 15 more locations, issued in the aftermath of border clashes in the Tawang sector.

April 2023: A third list named 11 locations, released amid a period of strained ties that included a visa dispute over Indian journalists based in China and international attention on India overtaking China as the world's most populous country.

March 2024: A fourth list added 30 locations, covering mountains, rivers and settlements, and took effect days before Prime Minister Modi inaugurated the Sela Tunnel, an all-weather high-altitude route improving troop mobility toward Tawang.

May 2025: A fifth list added 27 more locations.

April 2026: A sixth list renamed 23 locations, including 15 mountains, five residential areas, four mountain passes, two rivers and a lake, each assigned names in Chinese characters, Tibetan script and pinyin, complete with GPS coordinates.

Across the six lists, China has now assigned invented names to more than 90 locations in Arunachal Pradesh, covering villages and towns, mountain peaks and passes, land segments, rivers and lakes, in districts spanning most of the state. Each of these places already has an established Indian name in local, administrative and historical use. India has rejected every one of these lists, consistently, using close to identical language each time: Arunachal Pradesh "was, is, and will always remain" an Indian state, and assigning invented names "will not alter this reality."

Two things about the pattern deserve attention. First, the timing is rarely accidental. Several batches have followed events that visibly irritated Beijing, a Dalai Lama visit, a border clash, an Indian infrastructure milestone. Second, the scale has grown with each release, moving from six names to as many as 30 in a single list. Anushka Saxena, a research analyst at the Bengaluru-based Takshashila Institution, has described the exercise as "an exercise in rewriting history," arguing that repeated naming is intended to slowly normalize Chinese nomenclature in how the world refers to these places. Analysts have also linked this to China's broader adoption of the term "Xizang" for Tibet, which functions to reinforce the argument that Tibet was never independent, a foundational premise for the claim that Arunachal, as "Zangnan" or South Tibet, was never India's to begin with.

The colonial-era line China never accepted

To understand why China rejects a border most of the world treats as settled, it helps to go back to where that border came from.

The McMahon Line was drawn at the Simla Convention of 1913-14, an agreement between British India and Tibet that fixed the boundary between Tibet and British India's northeastern frontier. China's Republican government, represented at the talks, refused to sign the final convention, and Beijing has argued ever since that Tibet had no independent authority to conclude a boundary agreement on its own, meaning the line itself has no legal standing in Chinese eyes. This is the foundational legal position underneath everything else China does in Arunachal Pradesh: reject the McMahon Line, and the entire premise of the modern Indian border collapses, at least as Beijing frames it.

India's position rejects this reasoning entirely, treating the McMahon Line as a valid international boundary and Arunachal Pradesh as under Indian administration since well before independence, formalized through NEFA and later statehood in 1987.

1962: what actually happened, and why it matters for today's debate

The Sino-Indian War of October-November 1962 is frequently invoked in current arguments about the border, sometimes with more heat than accuracy, so the facts are worth stating precisely.

Chinese forces launched coordinated offensives on October 20, 1962, in both the eastern sector (NEFA, present-day Arunachal Pradesh) and the western sector (Aksai Chin, in Ladakh). In the east, Indian defenses were overwhelmed. Chinese troops captured Tawang, then Bomdila, and by late November had advanced close to the Assam plains near Tezpur. Prime Minister Jawaharlal Nehru's radio address at the time, describing "reverses" at Walong, Se La and Bomdila, reflected how serious the situation had become in the east.

On November 21, 1962, China declared a unilateral ceasefire and announced its forces would withdraw 20 kilometers behind the McMahon Line. "Unilateral" here describes the mechanism, China acted without a negotiated treaty or ceasefire agreement with India, not a claim that Indian resistance played no role anywhere along the front. Chinese forces withdrew to approximately pre-war positions by early December.

Why China withdrew remains debated among historians, and the explanations are not mutually exclusive. The most commonly cited factors are logistics, sustaining forces across Himalayan passes through the winter, at the end of long supply lines, was operationally difficult; and political signaling, Beijing appears to have wanted to demonstrate military superiority and force India toward negotiations on Chinese terms without committing to a long occupation, all while the war coincided almost exactly with the Cuban Missile Crisis, when neither global power wanted a second major flashpoint demanding attention.

A separate and important point of clarification: the Battle of Rezang La, fought by Major Shaitan Singh's C Company of 13 Kumaon on November 18, 1962, three days before the ceasefire, is one of the most storied last stands in Indian military history and rightly commands deep respect. But it was fought in the Chushul sector of Ladakh, the western front. It has no direct bearing on why China withdrew from NEFA in the east, a separate theatre more than a thousand kilometers away, where no comparable stand halted the Chinese advance before the ceasefire was announced. Conflating the two fronts is a common error in public discussion of 1962, and it matters for accuracy.

Not all territory captured in 1962 was returned. Small pockets in what is now Upper Subansiri district, including Longju, Asaphila, Bisa and Maja, remain under Chinese control to this day, distinct from the much larger loss of Aksai Chin in the western sector. The Indian government's current official position, stated by Minister of State for Home Kiren Rijiju in the Lok Sabha in 2025, is that China has not occupied any additional Arunachal territory since 1962, and that the areas it currently holds were already effectively under Chinese control before or during the war.

What China is actually after

Strip away the legal argument over the McMahon Line and four distinct strategic interests emerge, and they explain both the persistence of the claim and its intensity in specific places.

Tawang and the Dalai Lama's succession. Tawang holds genuine religious significance in Tibetan Buddhism, it is the birthplace of the sixth Dalai Lama and home to the second-largest Tibetan Buddhist monastery in the world, and this is the one part of Arunachal where China's cultural argument carries real historical weight rather than being a purely modern invention.

That significance has taken on new urgency. The 14th Dalai Lama, who turned 91 in July 2026, has stated that the institution of the Dalai Lama will continue after his death and that the Gaden Phodrang Trust, based in Dharamshala, holds sole authority to identify his successor, explicitly excluding Beijing from the process. He has further indicated his successor will not be born in Chinese-controlled territory but in a "free country," with India considered the most likely location, potentially even Tawang itself. China has rejected this framework outright, asserting since a 2007 State Religious Affairs Bureau order that any reincarnation of a "Living Buddha," including the Dalai Lama, requires approval by Beijing and adherence to the Qing-era Golden Urn ritual, and analysts widely expect China to name its own rival successor after the 14th Dalai Lama's death. If the next Dalai Lama is identified in or near Tawang, China's territorial claim and its religious legitimacy contest over Tibetan Buddhism become the same fight, which is why Tawang carries strategic weight for Beijing well beyond its size.

Water. Nearly all the major tributaries of the Brahmaputra originate in Arunachal Pradesh, and control of the state carries direct implications for water flowing downstream into Assam and, eventually, Bangladesh. China broke ground in July 2025 on the Motuo (Medog) Hydropower Station on the Yarlung Tsangpo, the river that becomes the Siang and then the Brahmaputra as it crosses into India. The project sits at the river's "Great Bend," where the water drops roughly 2,000 meters over about 50 kilometers, and is designed around five cascading dams with plans to divert as much as half the river's flow. At a planned capacity of 60 gigawatts and projected cost exceeding $137 billion, it is intended to generate roughly three times the output of the Three Gorges Dam, making it the largest and most expensive infrastructure project on the planet. Arunachal Pradesh Chief Minister Pema Khandu has publicly warned that the Siang and Brahmaputra could "dry up considerably" once the dam is operational and has called it "an existential threat" to the state's tribes and livelihoods. China does not need to physically hold Arunachal Pradesh to exercise this leverage, the dam sits entirely on its side of the border, but the project underscores why the region's river systems, which originate on Indian soil before the Yarlung Tsangpo even reaches China's dam site, matter so much to the wider water-security picture.

Strategic buffer and military terrain. For Beijing, the Arunachal claim is closely tied to consolidating control over Tibet itself. Holding, or credibly contesting, the high ground along this stretch of the Himalayas gives China observation over India's northeastern corridor and complicates Indian troop deployment and logistics. Arunachal functions as a defensive buffer for India; for China, pressing the claim ties down Indian forces on a second front alongside the existing Pakistan-facing deployment, a low-cost way of keeping India strategically stretched.

Cost-free pressure. Renaming places China has never governed costs Beijing almost nothing, no administration, no population to manage, no infrastructure to build, yet it forces India to spend diplomatic bandwidth on rejection statements, cartographic counter-moves and periodic reassurance to its own citizens in the state. Keeping the claim alive, rather than pursuing outright annexation, is the more sustainable long-term strategy, and the record of the past nine years bears that out.

Why a full invasion remains unlikely, but localized risk is real

The realistic military assessment does not point toward a repeat of 1962 at scale. India has spent the post-Galwan decade building forward infrastructure, including the Sela Tunnel, roads and airstrips, alongside a substantially higher forward troop density than existed in 1962. Any large Chinese move today risks a full conventional war between two nuclear-armed states, a fundamentally different calculation than in 1962. China also already secures its water leverage from its own side of the border, without needing to occupy Indian territory to do so.

What is more plausible is continued incremental pressure: further renaming lists, localized encroachment attempts of the kind reported near Upper Subansiri, and heightened friction specifically around Tawang, especially as the Dalai Lama succession question moves from a hypothetical to an active event. The December 2022 clash at Yangtse, also in the Tawang sector, is an illustration of where localized friction has occurred in the recent past.

India's position, unchanged

Successive Indian governments, across party lines, have used strikingly consistent language in response to every one of China's renaming exercises: Arunachal Pradesh is an integral and inalienable part of India, and assigning invented names does not alter that reality. External Affairs Minister S Jaishankar, responding to the March 2024 renaming batch, put it plainly: changing the name of a house does not make it yours. Arunachal Pradesh was an Indian state, is an Indian state, and will remain one.

The August 2026 episode marks a shift in tactics rather than substance. For the first time, India did not merely reject China's names, it published its own, permanently, on the official record. Whether that changes the trajectory of a nine-year pattern remains to be seen. What is unlikely to change is the position underneath it.
