Iran's Shadow War Reaches the Royal Family

A widening campaign of threats and plots on British soil collides with London's decision to keep hosting U.S. bombers bound for Iran.

Sagar Kashyap · 25 Jul 2026

LONDON — For years, Iran's confrontation with Britain played out in the shadows: foiled assassination plots, harassed journalists, and diplomatic sparring over a paramilitary force London was reluctant to formally blacklist. This week, it surfaced in the open. Iran's Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps issued a direct warning to the "British monarchy," naming a Royal Air Force base as a legitimate target and accusing the Royal Family's government of complicity in the U.S. bombing campaign against Iranian territory.

The warning is less a break from the pattern than its logical escalation — and it lands at the worst possible moment for a British government seven days into a new premiership.

The trigger

The immediate spark was a B-1 bomber mission that took off in the direction of Iran after refueling operations tied to RAF Fairford, the U.S. Air Force's only dedicated forward bomber base in Europe, located in Gloucestershire. Axios and other outlets reported it as the first UK-launched strike since fighting with Iran resumed roughly twelve days earlier, and the first since Andy Burnham took office as prime minister. By July 24, Reuters had counted a 13th consecutive night of U.S. strikes on Iranian targets, following the collapse of a roughly two-month truce after Iran struck commercial vessels that had bypassed an approved shipping corridor.

Fairford's role is not new. The Ministry of Defence began granting the U.S. access on March 6, after Prime Minister Keir Starmer authorized it on March 1 for what London described as a narrowly defensive purpose: destroying Iranian missiles "at source." At its peak, roughly 15 B-1B and several B-52H bombers were staged there — the largest concentration of American strategic bombers on British soil since the Cold War. Analysts have linked the shift toward bomber-launched strikes to the depletion of ship-based cruise missile stocks in the Indian Ocean after nearly five months of continuous operations.

Fairford is not the only British-linked installation in Iran's crosshairs. Diego Garcia, the joint U.S.-U.K. base in the Indian Ocean, was targeted by two Iranian ballistic missiles in March, both of which missed. RAF Akrotiri in Cyprus was struck by a Shahed-type drone the same month.

The warning

The Royal Family threat arrived as a coda to a statement primarily about a different target. On July 23, the IRGC's public relations office issued what it labeled Announcement No. 46, detailing a retaliatory strike on U.S. forces at Al-Udairi base in Kuwait — described as "Wave 26" of an operation the IRGC said was launched after a U.S. strike that morning on pilgrims traveling to Karbala for Arbaeen. It was only in the statement's closing section, distributed via the IRGC's Telegram channel, that Iran turned to Britain: reasserting that any base facilitating aggression against Iranian territory counts as a legitimate target, then directly addressing the "British monarchy" and accusing it of historic responsibility for regional instability — citing, among other grievances, Britain's role in the founding of Israel — before warning London against deepening its role in the present campaign.

British outlets including GB News and the Daily Mirror, which first surfaced the translated text, reported that the threat is widely read as aimed at Britain's political leadership rather than King Charles or the royal household personally. No attack on U.K. soil has been claimed; the statement is a warning tied to continued base access, not a claimed operation.

London's answer

The government's response came not from Burnham but through a Ministry of Defence spokesperson, who said Britain's armed forces stand ready to keep the country safe "whether it's on our soil or from abroad," pointing to a layered air and missile defense posture spanning the Royal Navy, British Army and RAF, coordinated with NATO allies. Asked directly about the Fairford claims, the government declined to comment, citing operational security.

Substantively, Burnham has changed nothing. Within days of taking office he confirmed continued U.S. access to Fairford and Diego Garcia, maintaining Starmer's policy rather than reopening it. He has since spoken with President Trump by phone, with Downing Street noting Britain's "commitment to securing the movement of shipping in the Strait of Hormuz"; Trump described the call as having "went very well," citing discussion of trade, defense cooperation and demining the Strait. Domestically, the continuity has drawn fire: the Green Party and Liberal Democrats argue base access makes Britain complicit in potential war crimes, and Human Rights Watch's U.K. director has written to Burnham raising legal concerns tied to a Pentagon-confirmed strike on a school in Minab, Iran, in February.

The pattern beneath the headline

The Royal Family warning is dramatic, but it sits atop a threat picture British intelligence had already flagged as historic in scale. A report from Parliament's Intelligence and Security Committee, published the same week as the IRGC statement, concluded that Iran's physical threat to the U.K. is now comparable to Russia's — citing at least 15 attempted murders or kidnappings of British nationals or residents since 2022. Committee chairman Lord Beamish described Iran as having "a high appetite for risk when conducting offensive activity" on foreign soil, backed by intelligence services he called "ferociously well resourced with significant areas of asymmetric strength."

Iran has "a high appetite for risk when conducting offensive activity" on foreign soil, backed by intelligence services that are "ferociously well resourced with significant areas of asymmetric strength."

The committee's evidence includes cases like that of British-Iranian journalist Sima Sabet, who was informed in late 2023 she was the target of an assassination plot and was issued a panic button by counter-terrorism police; police later told her they had intelligence her life was in danger inside her own home and asked her to leave immediately. Family members of BBC Persian journalists still living in Iran have separately reported interrogation and threats tied to relatives' work abroad.

The pressure to formally proscribe the IRGC as a terrorist organization under the Terrorism Act 2000 has been building for over a year — the London Assembly called for it in March, citing more than twenty credible threats including a foiled plot against the Israeli Embassy in Kensington. Britain had long resisted, with ministers arguing that banning the IRGC would sever a direct diplomatic channel to Tehran useful for de-escalation. That calculus appears to have shifted: reporting this month indicates the government has moved to restrict support for the group under new national security legislation, even as it maintains the base-access arrangement Iran now cites as grounds for retaliation.

The bottom line

Britain finds itself holding two positions simultaneously — hardening against Iran's covert threat network at home while keeping its airfields open to the bombing campaign Tehran says justifies that network's expansion. For Burnham, five days into confronting his first major foreign-policy test, the royal-family warning is unlikely to change British policy. But it sharpens the question Parliament's own intelligence committee has already put in writing: whether a threat picture once managed quietly through counter-terrorism policing is now operating on the same footing as a hostile state.

Sourcing: Reuters, Axios, UK Ministry of Defence statements, UK Parliament Intelligence and Security Committee report, London Assembly records, and IRGC statements as reported via WANA news agency, GB News and the Daily Mirror.
