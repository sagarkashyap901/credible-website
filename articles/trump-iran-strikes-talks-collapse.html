Trump Runs Out of Patience on Iran, Orders New Strikes as Talks Collapse

Energy and infrastructure targets in the crosshairs; officials say Israel could join for the first time in weeks, raising fears of a fresh missile war.

Sagar Kashyap · 2 Aug 2026

WASHINGTON — President Trump has told advisers he is done negotiating with Tehran and has ordered the U.S. military to prepare a fresh wave of strikes on Iranian energy infrastructure that could begin within days, according to U.S. officials familiar with the planning — a sharp reversal after months of on-again, off-again diplomacy meant to end the war that erupted five months ago.

The shift caps a week of deteriorating talks and reflects what people close to the president describe as genuine exasperation. Trump has told confidants he is in "revenge mode" against Tehran and has grown convinced that the Islamic Republic understands nothing but force, according to a senior administration official. He has soured on the negotiating track pursued for months by Middle East envoy Steve Witkoff and White House adviser Jared Kushner, even as the two continue to argue internally for giving diplomacy more room to work.

"Well, we'll be hitting them very hard, and, you know, at some point they're going to say, we just can't take it anymore," Trump told reporters at the start of a Cabinet meeting Friday. He added that the more the U.S. strikes Iran, the weaker its government gets, "and then they peter out."

White House press secretary Karoline Leavitt said Friday that "the United States will win, and Iran will not have a nuclear weapon under his watch."

A U.S. official told Axios that final orders for the strikes had not yet been given as of Friday, and that the operation — which would target Iranian energy and infrastructure sites — could still be called off. But planning is far enough along that officials describe a launch window of days, not weeks. Notably, the plan under discussion could involve the Israeli military directly for the first time in many weeks, a step that would mark a significant escalation and one that American officials acknowledge would likely draw a new round of Iranian missile fire at Israel.

A war five months in

The confrontation traces back to Feb. 28, when the U.S. and Israel launched a campaign of strikes against Iran's nuclear and ballistic-missile programs — an operation that also killed Iran's supreme leader, Ayatollah Ali Khamenei. Tehran responded by attacking American bases across the region, striking Israel directly, and closing the Strait of Hormuz, the corridor through which roughly a fifth of the world's seaborne oil passes. Iranian missiles and drones also hit Qatar, Saudi Arabia, Jordan and the United Arab Emirates in the weeks that followed, while Iran-backed Hezbollah opened a front from Lebanon that drew an Israeli ground response.

A shaky de-escalation took hold in early spring. Pakistan brokered a two-week pause in early April, and Vice President JD Vance held direct talks in Islamabad with Iran's parliament speaker, Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf — the highest-level face-to-face contact between Washington and Tehran since the 1979 revolution. Those talks produced a 14-point memorandum of understanding, signed June 17, laying out a 60-day framework to address Hormuz shipping, Iran's nuclear and missile programs, and sanctions relief.

That framework has frayed repeatedly. In late March, Trump had already threatened to bomb Iran's power plants over the Hormuz closure, then twice postponed the strikes for five days at a time, citing what he called "productive" conversations — claims Iranian state media disputed at the time. By July, officials on both sides were describing the June memorandum as effectively dead, with Trump at one point declaring the ceasefire "over" even as backchannel talks continued.

Inside the administration, the path forward has divided Trump's own team. Vance, Witkoff and Kushner have pushed to keep the diplomatic track alive; Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, Secretary of State Marco Rubio and CIA Director John Ratcliffe have been more skeptical, according to people familiar with the internal debate. Trump himself has increasingly sided with the hawks, telling associates the Iranians are "liars and lunatics" who wouldn't honor a deal even if one were signed. He has specifically cited frustration with the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps' negotiating style, which he has likened to haggling with a rug merchant, according to the Journal's reporting.

Why energy infrastructure

The targets under consideration — oil and gas facilities, refineries, and power plants — are chosen deliberately. Unlike strikes on military or nuclear sites, hitting the energy grid squeezes the revenue and domestic stability the Iranian government depends on, giving Washington leverage it hopes will force Tehran back to the table on U.S. terms rather than its own. Trump road-tested the same threat in March, when the mere prospect of strikes on power plants was enough to buy several days of talks. Officials now describe the calculus as similar, but with far less patience behind it: this time, the administration is prepared to follow through.

The risk is that Iran, its government already under pressure after the killing of its supreme leader and mounting domestic anger, responds not by capitulating but by escalating — hitting Israel, U.S. forces in the Gulf, or shipping lanes again. Iranian officials have publicly vowed retaliation, and billboards calling for revenge for Khamenei's death have appeared in Tehran in recent days.

What's next

U.S. officials caution that the operation could still be delayed or scaled back, as has happened before. But the mood in Washington has changed. Where earlier deadlines came with an implicit off-ramp, officials now describe Trump as looking for an excuse to act rather than a reason to wait. Messages relayed through intermediary governments suggest Iran is aware a new strike is imminent; how — or whether — Tehran responds diplomatically before any bombs fall may determine whether the war's fragile ceasefire framework survives at all.
