Trump Pulls Back From Iran Strikes, Citing Progress Toward a Deal

The reversal came hours after Kuwait intercepted a fresh Iranian drone barrage and a call from Saudi Arabia's crown prince urging restraint. Any pause is contingent on a deal covering Hormuz and Iran's nuclear program, and the region has been here before.

Sagar Kashyap · 2 Aug 2026

President Trump said Saturday night that he is canceling a planned attack on Iran, citing progress in talks, just a day after vowing to hit Tehran "very hard" and ordering the Pentagon to prepare a fresh wave of strikes as soon as this weekend. In a statement posted by the White House, Trump said the U.S. remains "locked and loaded and ready to go" but had agreed to hold off after being asked to do so by Iran and other Middle Eastern countries, on the understanding that the outline of a deal was in place. The pause, he said, is contingent on being able to move quickly to a final agreement, one that he said must include reopening the Strait of Hormuz and ending Iran's nuclear weapons program.

The announcement capped a whiplash 48 hours. Trump had ordered strike preparations Friday and used unusually blunt language about the scale of force under consideration. Early Saturday, Kuwait's Defense Ministry said it intercepted a wave of Iranian drones that struck a government facility in the country's north, causing damage but no casualties, according to spokesman Maj. Gen. Saud Abdulaziz Al-Otaibi. Hours later, an LNG tanker carrying Qatari gas, the Gaslog Shanghai, was hit by a projectile while transiting Hormuz off the Omani coast, UK Maritime Trade Operations said. The U.S. State Department issued fresh travel warnings covering ten countries in the region, from Bahrain to the UAE, and American embassies urged citizens to consider their exposure.

It was against that backdrop that Saudi Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman called Trump on Saturday, reinforcing Riyadh's preference for de-escalation and raising concerns directly about the strike plans, according to people familiar with the call cited by CNN. That appeal, combined with similar requests from other regional governments, is what Trump pointed to in explaining the reversal.

A deal that still has to be built

Trump's own framing set a high bar for what would count as success: an agreement that reopens Hormuz to normal shipping and ends Iran's nuclear weapons program, two demands Tehran has resisted conceding simultaneously at every previous negotiating juncture this year. Iran's government has not confirmed the terms Trump described, and there was no immediate comment from Iranian officials or mediators on the substance of what had allegedly been agreed in "concept and detail."

Tehran's own posture over the same 48 hours was mixed at best. Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi had warned days earlier that Iran's defense doctrine amounts to an "eye for an eye" against any strikes on its infrastructure, and a senior Iranian commander, Ali Abdollahi, separately warned Gulf states hosting U.S. forces that they would "burn in the fires of war" if the campaign continued. Iran conducted the Kuwait drone strike and was linked to the Hormuz tanker attack on the very day its government was reportedly asking Washington for a pause, a contradiction that points to either a fractured decision-making process in Tehran or a deliberate attempt to negotiate from a position of demonstrated capability.

Why Trump blinked, or says he did

Domestic politics is doing real work here. An AP-NORC poll released this week found roughly two-thirds of American adults do not believe the war with Iran has been worth fighting, a view shared broadly across Democrats and independents and by more than a third of Republicans. Barely one in five adults wants the U.S. to continue military action; a similar share wants it stopped entirely, with the rest favoring a pause for renewed negotiations. That is a difficult backdrop for any president weighing a further escalation, particularly one explicitly framed in Trump's own words as force "not seen since World War II."

This is also not the first time Trump has announced an imminent breakthrough only for the fighting to resume days later. In February, oil prices fell sharply after Trump said he was "hopeful" about a deal, before the war began days after that. In March, he postponed a threatened strike on Iranian power plants for five days citing "very good and productive" talks, a pause Iran's own foreign ministry dismissed at the time as a stalling tactic. In June, Washington and Tehran signed a memorandum of understanding that briefly cratered oil prices, only for Trump to declare the agreement "over" in July after Iranian strikes on tankers, sending Brent up more than 6% in a single session. Each cycle has followed a similar shape: threat, brief diplomatic opening, a fresh Iranian strike somewhere in the Gulf, and a return to escalation. Markets that have been burned repeatedly this year are likely to treat Saturday's announcement with some of that same caution.

Markets

Brent crude had climbed toward $88 a barrel on Friday and Saturday as the strike threat built, extending a monthly gain of more than 20%, its best month since March, according to trading data. Whether that gives back ground now depends on how durable this pause proves to be. Given the pattern above, a single Iranian strike anywhere in the Gulf in the coming days would likely be enough to reverse any relief rally.

The India exposure

None of this changes India's underlying position, and if anything it sharpens the stakes riding on whether Trump's "rapid deal" actually materializes. Roughly 40% of India's crude imports, 60% of its LNG and close to 90% of its LPG normally move through the Middle East and the Strait of Hormuz, according to India's petroleum ministry. The rupee has weakened past 92 to the dollar and India's crude import bill has risen sharply this fiscal year, both consequences of five months of Gulf disruption. New Delhi invoked the Essential Commodities Act in March to protect household gas supply and has since leaned harder on U.S., Russian, Guyanese and Brazilian crude while signaling plans to expand strategic reserves, per Bloomberg reporting.

Hormuz's reopening was the one concrete, measurable term Trump attached to any deal. If that happens, it is the single most important data point for India's energy import bill and the rupee this year. If it does not, and history this year suggests skepticism is warranted, India's exposure through crude, LNG, LPG and the Iran-facing leg of the INSTC corridor stays exactly where it was Friday.

What to watch

Whether Tehran issues any public confirmation of the terms Trump described, particularly on the nuclear file, which has been the specific sticking point behind every prior collapsed truce this year.

Whether Iranian-linked strikes on Gulf shipping or Gulf states continue in the coming days. A repeat of Saturday's Kuwait or Hormuz incidents would likely be read as Tehran negotiating and striking simultaneously, undermining the basis for the pause.

The clock Trump himself set. He tied the cancellation explicitly to a "rapid" deal, not an open-ended one, which leaves the door open to a resumption of strike planning on short notice if talks stall.
