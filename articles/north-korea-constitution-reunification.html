The Country That Refused to Die: How North Korea Survived Every Prediction of Its Collapse

American intelligence spent four decades predicting the regime's collapse. In March 2026, Pyongyang quietly rewrote its founding document to define itself as a permanent, bounded state that excludes South Korea. The four forces that were supposed to destroy North Korea have each been neutralised, though not as cleanly as Kim Jong Un would like the world to believe.

Sagar Kashyap · 5 Aug 2026

On January 21, 1998, the Central Intelligence Agency circulated an internal report titled "Exploring the Implications of Alternative North Korean Endgames: Results from a Discussion Panel on Continuing Coexistence Between North and South Korea." A partially declassified version, with pages excised, was later released under the Freedom of Information Act and is held by the National Security Archive at George Washington University.

The report was the product of a sustained analytical effort. According to the National Security Archive's summary, the CIA had convened a panel in March 1997 to examine three Korean endgame scenarios: a limited North Korean invasion of the South, a coup and ensuing civil war in the North, and peaceful unification under Seoul's leadership. A second roundtable of outside experts and government officials met in late 1997 to assess the prospects for reconciliation and for a prolonged period of competitive coexistence. The 1998 document reports the findings of that second panel, which the Archive notes was dominated by a single variable: the question of time, meaning whether North Korea could survive at all.

It concluded that the regime probably could not. In a 2015 analysis of the declassified record, the historian Bruce Cumings noted that the panel assessed North Korea as likely to collapse within roughly five years, and that the assembled experts believed Kim Jong Il had only a brief window of time to manage the crisis he had inherited.

This was not an outlier view. It was the settled position of the American national security establishment. Cumings documents that CIA Director John Deutch told Congress in 1995 that it was no longer a question of whether North Korea would collapse but when, and that General Gary Luck, then commander of US forces in Korea, said in 1997 that North Korea would disintegrate, possibly very quickly, with the only open question being whether it would implode or explode.

Nor was 1998 the first attempt. CIA records released through the agency's FOIA reading room show that in October 1987, the Director of Central Intelligence's Senior Review Panel was reviewing terms of reference for National Intelligence Estimate 14.2-87, titled "North Korea: Prospects for the Succession and Internal Stability," which addressed the challenges facing Kim Il Sung's effort to install his son in power. Washington had been forecasting the regime's succession-driven unravelling for four decades.

One clarification matters for anyone working from this record. The declassified document does not present a tidy numbered list of collapse causes. What emerges instead, when the 1998 assessment is read alongside the wider intelligence and academic literature of the period, is a set of four distinct structural threats. Each was, at the time, plausibly fatal. Each has since been addressed.

The trap built into the founding

Before the four threats, there is the deeper problem that made all of them worse.

Korea was a single polity for centuries before Japan annexed it in 1910. After Japan's defeat in 1945, the peninsula was divided at the 38th parallel as an administrative convenience between American and Soviet occupation forces. Two governments emerged in 1948, each claiming to be the legitimate authority over the entire peninsula. The 1950 to 1953 war ended in an armistice rather than a peace treaty, which froze the division without settling the question. Both states retained reunification as constitutional doctrine. The split was always officially temporary.

That was survivable while the two Koreas were comparably poor. It became lethal once they were not. As South Korea industrialised and then democratised, the economic gap widened past the point of comparison, and any real unification could only mean absorption of the North by the South. North Korea's founding promise had become a countdown against its own existence. The Korea Economic Institute of America, in its May 2026 analysis of the constitutional revision, framed the historical scale of this bluntly: Germany and Austria were divided after the Second World War under broadly similar circumstances and reunified decades ago, while the Korean division has now lasted considerably longer than either.

Threat one: destruction by the United States

When the Soviet Union dissolved in 1991, North Korea lost its guarantor overnight. Subsidised Soviet oil, food and security cover vanished at a moment when the United States was the sole global superpower and was demonstrably willing to use force. American planners actively considered strikes on the Yongbyon nuclear complex during the 1994 crisis. North Korea's conventional forces were large but obsolete. It had no patron prepared to fight for it.

The solution was nuclear. North Korea conducted its first test in 2006 and five more through 2017, the last of which Pyongyang claimed as a thermonuclear device. In parallel it built out an intercontinental ballistic missile programme, the Hwasong-14 and Hwasong-15 in 2017, later the Hwasong-17 and the solid-fuel Hwasong-18, with demonstrated range sufficient to reach the continental United States.

The strategic consequence is straightforward. Once Washington could not guarantee that a preventive strike would not be answered with a nuclear detonation on an American city, preventive war ceased to be a serious policy instrument. Nuclear weapons are not a bargaining chip for Pyongyang, which is why every negotiation premised on trading them away has failed. They are the mechanism by which the first threat was permanently closed.

The programme has not slowed. Euronews reported in May 2026 that Kim has continued to pledge expansion of his nuclear forces, and that Pyongyang conducted four missile tests in April 2026, the highest single-month total in more than two years.

Threat two: dependency on China

Beginning with Resolution 2270 in 2016, the UN Security Council passed a succession of resolutions targeting North Korea's export sectors. The Diplomat, drawing on trade data, reported that the resolutions collectively banned roughly 90 percent of North Korean exports in dollar terms, and that exports to China fell from $2.6 billion in 2016 to $213.2 million in 2018. Pandemic border closures pushed them further, to $48 million in 2020.

What sanctions did not do was diversify North Korea's economy. They concentrated it. Analysis published by 38 North found that since the 2016 to 2017 sanctions round, China has accounted for around 95 percent of the world's reported merchandise trade with North Korea. A July 2024 assessment by Stephan Haggard and Liuya Zhang for the National Committee on North Korea put the figure at perhaps as high as 95 percent, while noting that all such estimates rely on mirror statistics from trading partners because Pyongyang treats basic economic data as a state secret. The Foundation for Strategic Research, citing Council on Foreign Relations work, put China's share of North Korean foreign trade at approximately 98 percent in 2024.

That is not a partnership. It is a chokehold, and one that sits badly with a state whose founding ideology is self-reliance. The Foundation for Strategic Research notes that the relationship has long been underpinned by mutual distrust, particularly since Xi Jinping took power in 2012, and that Xi and Kim did not meet at all until 2018.

Two moves loosened the grip. The first was diplomatic. Nuclear status bought Kim direct summit meetings with a sitting American president in Singapore in 2018, in Hanoi in 2019 and at the Demilitarized Zone later that year, conferring a form of international standing that Chinese aid could not.

The second was the Ukraine war, which is examined below. Its strategic effect was to give Pyongyang a second great-power patron, and therefore leverage over both. The evidence that this worked is visible in Chinese behaviour. Reuters reported in January 2026, citing Chinese customs data, that two-way China to North Korea trade rose 25 percent in 2025 to $2.73 billion, close to the $2.79 billion recorded in 2019, after falling 5 percent in 2024. Reuters linked the recovery to warming relations, noting that Xi held his first meeting with Kim in six years when the North Korean leader visited Beijing in September 2025, and that questions about the relationship's future had surfaced precisely because of Pyongyang's deepening military cooperation with Moscow.

Beijing, in other words, moved to re-engage after Kim demonstrated he had an alternative.

Threat three: economic collapse

The famine of roughly 1994 to 1998, known inside North Korea as the Arduous March, remains the reference point. The public distribution system failed, industry seized, and the countryside was largely without reliable power. Credible mortality estimates vary by an order of magnitude, from around 240,000 to over three million, with most rigorous scholarship clustering between 600,000 and one million deaths in a population then numbering roughly 22 million.

The economy never fully recovered, and the 2020 to 2022 border closure compounded the damage. A 2026 study in the Journal of Government and Economics decomposing North Korea's trade with China over 1998 to 2024 found that export volumes crashed after 2017 as comprehensive UN sanctions took effect, fell to near zero during the 2020 to 2022 closure, and recovered only modestly toward 2024. Reuters' reading of Chinese customs data underlines how thin the legitimate economy remains: North Korea's single largest export category to China in 2025 was wigs and hair products, worth $190.55 million.

What changed the arithmetic was the war in Ukraine.

Estimates of what Pyongyang has earned from supplying Russia vary enormously, and the variance is itself analytically important. South Korea's Institute for National Security Strategy, in a March 2026 report by senior research fellow Lim Soo Ho, estimated total earnings of between $7.67 billion and $14.4 billion from August 2023 to December 2025. The breakdown attributes roughly $620 million to troop deployments and between $7.05 billion and $13.78 billion to exports of artillery, ammunition and guided and ballistic missiles, averaging around $10.4 billion. The Korea Institute for Defense Analyses has assessed the figure at above $20 billion. An earlier academic study by Olena Guseinova of Hankuk University of Foreign Studies put the arms deal alone at between $1.72 billion and $5.52 billion.

The physical volumes are not seriously disputed. The National Committee on North Korea assesses that Pyongyang has sent at least 20,000 shipping containers carrying an estimated eight million artillery shells, predominantly 122mm and 152mm, and that Western and Ukrainian intelligence assess North Korean ammunition accounted for 50 to 60 percent of what Russia fired in Ukraine and close to one third of Russian ballistic missile launches. South Korean intelligence figures cited by the Korea Institute for Defense Analyses run higher still, at over 12 million 152mm shells across roughly 28,000 containers.

The manpower is equally real. Seoul's intelligence services assess deployments began in October 2024, with large-scale waves in January 2025, August 2025 and from September through December 2025, totalling over 20,000 personnel serving in mine clearance, construction, engineering, logistics and support to Russian defence production including drone assembly. Those troops have died. South Korea's National Intelligence Service, cited by NK News, estimates around 2,000 North Korean soldiers killed.

And North Korea is building for the long term. Reporting on the relationship notes that in June 2025 Pyongyang began construction of its largest-ever military manufacturing facility near Huichon, expanding arms production for Russian use. That is a state investing capital on the assumption the arrangement outlives the war.

Here, however, is the qualification that most accounts of North Korea's revival omit.

The money may not have arrived. The Institute for National Security Strategy report, citing work by the Friedrich Naumann Foundation, found that only 4 to 19.6 percent of estimated earnings have been confirmed as actually received. The Naumann-commissioned study estimated that against as much as $9.8 billion in weapons, ammunition and personnel delivered since 2023, Russian compensation amounted to roughly $1.2 billion in food, oil, air defence systems, GPS jammers and possibly aircraft. The study characterised the arrangement as an unequal partnership in which North Korea's economy remains under strain and has seen little improvement.

Either Kim has been comprehensively underpaid by a partner with all the leverage, or the compensation is arriving in forms that do not appear in trade statistics — missile guidance, submarine, satellite and air defence technology. Both readings are consistent with the evidence.

The visible consumer transformation, meanwhile, has a separate and largely Chinese explanation. Research published by 38 North and NK TechLab finds that at least 24 domestic smartphone brands now compete in North Korea, with the proliferation beginning around 2023, and that state services including medicine delivery and ration coupons have moved online. Critically, the same research finds that all these handsets appear to be produced by Chinese original equipment manufacturers and shipped to North Korea, where each brand installs a security-modified variant of Android. There is no meaningful domestic production. Penetration is nonetheless higher than commonly assumed: 38 North's interviews with recent escapees suggest smartphone ownership between 50 and 80 percent of the adult population, while South Korean Ministry of Unification defector interview data indicates 71.2 percent ownership in Pyongyang against 31.1 percent in border areas.

The pattern extends to vehicles. North Korea has showcased a domestically branded electric car widely assessed to be a rebadged Chinese BYD Han, with the manufacturer's badge covered.

The accurate causal chain is therefore two-track. Chinese supply chains and the post-pandemic reopening produced the visible consumer economy. Russian war revenue is a separate, later and far larger flow, most of which appears to remain uncollected in cash terms.

Threat four: political instability

The 1987 and 1998 intelligence assessments were both, at root, about succession. There was no precedent for hereditary transfer of power in a communist state, and Western analysts doubted it could be sustained.

The doubts intensified with the second transfer. When Kim Jong Il died in December 2011, Kim Jong Un took power at roughly 27 years of age, Swiss-educated, publicly unknown until 2010 and visibly flanked by older regents. The working assumption in Seoul and Washington was that he would be a figurehead controlled by the military establishment, or would be removed by it.

He was neither. Ri Yong Ho, the army chief of staff, was stripped of his positions in July 2012. In December 2013, Kim's uncle by marriage Jang Song Thaek, widely regarded as the real centre of power, was denounced, tried and executed. In February 2017, Kim Jong Nam, the leader's half-brother and the only living alternative dynastic claimant, was assassinated with VX nerve agent at Kuala Lumpur International Airport. Hundreds of officials were purged across the decade.

Purges alone do not produce stability, however. They produce fear, which is a wasting asset. What consolidated the position was the combination of coercion with material reward: an elite that benefits from the status quo has no incentive to gamble on a successor.

The academic literature on regime durability supports this reading. Research by Erica Frantz and Andrea Kendall-Taylor examining all 79 cases of dictators dying in office between 1946 and 2012 found the ruling regime remained intact through the following year 87 percent of the time, against just 43 percent for all other forms of leadership exit in autocracies. Their finding is that dictators who survive to die in office have, by definition, built an inner circle invested in the perks of continuity, and that the same strategies that keep a leader in power until death also make the regime resilient after it.

On lifespan, the comparative data is worth stating carefully because the numbers in circulation conflate different things. Individual dictators average roughly 13 years in power according to Oxford's Department of Politics and International Relations, with personalist dictators averaging around ten years and military dictators only three to four. Regimes, as distinct from leaders, last longer: comparative work covering 1800 to 2000 puts the average autocracy at around 21 years and hybrid regimes at nine.

North Korea's system is in its 78th year. It has already outlasted the Soviet Union, which survived 69.

The constitutional rewrite

Which brings us to the document itself.

The sequence began in December 2023, when Kim denounced South Korea as a forward military base and nuclear arsenal of the United States and stated that he no longer believed unification could be achieved peacefully. On January 15, 2024, addressing the Supreme People's Assembly, he formally abandoned the goal, declaring that Pyongyang no longer regarded Seoul as a partner for reconciliation and reunification but as an enemy to be subjugated. He instructed that the constitution be revised to name the South the primary foe and invariable principal enemy, and said it could specify the occupation, subjugation and reclamation of the Republic of Korea, annexing it as territory, in the event of war.

The revision itself was adopted at a Supreme People's Assembly meeting in March 2026 but was not publicly known until early May. On May 6, 2026, a draft reviewed by Reuters and by AFP was presented at a briefing at South Korea's Unification Ministry by Professor Lee Jung Chul of Seoul National University.

What it contains is more consequential than what it removes, though both matter.

Removed: the clause committing North Korea to realise the unification of the motherland, and the associated language on national reunification on the principles of independence, peaceful reunification and great national unity. Yonhap's review of the text confirms that all references to reunification, including the phrases peaceful reunification and great national unity, have been eliminated. Yonhap further reports that the revision dropped the word socialist from the constitution's title and removed the enumerated achievements of Kim Il Sung and Kim Jong Il.

Added: for the first time in the history of the document, a territorial clause. Reuters and AFP both report that it defines North Korean territory as the land bordering China and Russia to the north and the Republic of Korea to the south, together with adjacent territorial waters and airspace, and states that North Korea will not tolerate any infringement of that territory. The Korea Economic Institute of America notes that where the previous constitution defined the country as a people-centred socialist state representing the interests of the Korean people, the amended document is oriented around specific territory from which South Korea is explicitly excluded.

Yonhap also reports that the revision elevated the leader's status, designating the president of the State Affairs Commission as head of state and placing him above the Supreme People's Assembly.

Read together, this is a state re-founding itself. It has stopped defining itself as one half of a temporarily divided nation and started defining itself as a bounded, permanent country with fixed borders, a head of state and no irredentist claim. It has also, in dropping the founders' achievements from the text, quietly made itself Kim Jong Un's state rather than his grandfather's.

The counter-reading

There is a less triumphal interpretation, and serious analysts hold it.

At the same Unification Ministry briefing, Professor Lee Jung Chul assessed the revision positively, arguing that it signals Pyongyang's intent to project itself as a normal state, noting the absence of hostile language toward Seoul in the constitutional text and suggesting it could lay groundwork for peaceful coexistence.

There are also unresolved edges. Reuters notes that the territorial clause does not specify the location of the border with South Korea and does not address disputed maritime boundaries such as the Northern Limit Line in the Yellow Sea, which is precisely where inter-Korean naval clashes have historically occurred. A constitution that claims territorial inviolability while leaving the contested maritime boundary undefined has not settled the question so much as deferred it.

And the timing is pointed. AFP reports that South Korean President Lee Jae Myung has called for talks without preconditions, saying the two countries are destined to make the flowers of peace bloom, and that Pyongyang has not responded and has repeatedly called the South its most hostile adversary. One side is still reaching toward a goal the other has legally abolished.

The strongest version of the sceptical case is this: Kim did not abandon reunification because he won. He abandoned it because he finally conceded he could never win it. Locking a door is what you do when the contest is over, not when you have taken the prize.

What this means for India

Three implications warrant attention from New Delhi.

The sanctions precedent. The Institute for National Security Strategy's own conclusion is the most consequential line in the entire body of analysis: if North Korea fully collects compensation for troop deployments and arms exports, the core economic effect of sanctions on Pyongyang, the restriction of foreign currency inflows, would be neutralised. The most comprehensive multilateral sanctions regime ever constructed has been substantially defeated, not through evasion at the margins but because a permanent Security Council member became a paying customer. That is the same structural logic operating across the Russian energy trade, and India has direct experience of the pressure that follows.

Proliferation drift. Whatever missile, submarine, satellite and air defence technology Moscow transfers to Pyongyang does not stay in Pyongyang. North Korean systems and know-how have historically circulated through the same networks that shape the Chinese and Pakistani technology ecosystem that Indian defence planning must account for. The Friedrich Naumann study found no firm evidence of direct Russian advancement of North Korean defence technology, though Moscow has been linked to construction of a new 5,000-tonne North Korean warship. The absence of confirmed transfer is not the same as its absence, and this is a file worth watching closely rather than assuming settled.

The durability lesson. The wider point is not about Korea. It is that a small, sanctioned, resource-poor autocracy survived four decades of confident great-power predictions of its demise by acquiring a nuclear deterrent, refusing to trade it, and waiting for the international system to produce a buyer for what it had to sell. That lesson is legible to every state currently weighing whether the costs of a nuclear programme exceed the costs of doing without one. It is the least discussed and most destabilising consequence of North Korea's survival.

The unresolved question

Four threats, four answers. War was closed by nuclear weapons. Dependency was loosened by acquiring a second patron. Political instability was ended by purge and then by patronage. Economic collapse was averted, or at least postponed, by a war in Europe that needed shells and bodies at a moment when North Korea had a surplus of both.

But the fourth answer is the shakiest, and it is contingent on a war that will eventually end. When it does, Russian demand for North Korean artillery falls away, and Pyongyang is left holding an enormous unpaid invoice, a partner with no further need of it and a Chinese relationship it spent three years demonstrating it could live without.

The CIA's 1998 panel was wrong about the timeline. It may not have been wrong about the mechanism. North Korea has not solved the problem of being a small, poor state in a hostile neighbourhood. It has found a series of increasingly ingenious ways to defer it. The constitution now says the country is permanent. Constitutions have said stranger things.
