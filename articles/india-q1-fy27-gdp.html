India Grew 7.8 Percent. The Growth Was Broad. The Deflator Is the Question.

Investment, manufacturing, services, consumption and exports all contributed in April to June. The harder questions are about a GDP deflator far below wholesale inflation, a deficient monsoon, and whether the quarter's momentum survives the second half of the year.

Sagar Kashyap · 31 Aug 2026

India's economy grew 7.8 percent in the April to June quarter of FY2026-27, the Ministry of Statistics and Programme Implementation reported on Monday. Real GDP reached Rs 81.36 lakh crore against Rs 75.46 lakh crore a year earlier. Nominal GDP grew 10.3 percent to Rs 88.27 lakh crore. Real gross value added grew 8.2 percent, nominal GVA 11.5 percent.

The result beat a Reuters poll of 58 economists projecting 7.1 percent, a Moneycontrol survey median of 7.3 percent, and the Reserve Bank of India's own forecast of 7 percent. Prime Minister Narendra Modi called it a herculean feat. Finance Minister Nirmala Sitharaman credited reforms and economic management.

The quarter was good, and it was good in more places than a sceptical reading would predict. That is the honest starting point. The questions worth asking are not about whether the economy grew but about how the growth was priced, how durable it is, and what the number does and does not tell us about employment.

This piece separates three things throughout: what the data establish, what is a reasonable reading of the data, and what remains genuinely open.

What the data establish

Growth was broad on the production side. The secondary sector grew 8.6 percent and the tertiary sector 10 percent. Manufacturing grew 9.2 percent against 8.3 percent a year earlier. Financial, real estate, IT and professional services grew 12.1 percent. The primary sector was the laggard at 2.9 percent, with agriculture and allied activities at 3.6 percent.

Two arithmetic points follow directly and are worth stating because they cut against a lazy reading. A tertiary sector growing 10 percent overall, with its fastest component at 12.1 percent, means the rest of services, including the labour-intensive trade, hotels, transport and communication block, also grew strongly. Similarly, a secondary sector at 8.6 percent with manufacturing at 9.2 percent means construction and utilities together contributed materially. This was not a quarter carried by one elite sector.

Every major expenditure component was positive. Gross fixed capital formation grew 11.9 percent in real terms against 5.8 percent a year earlier. Private final consumption expenditure grew 7.1 percent and made up 55.8 percent of nominal GDP. Exports of goods and services grew 12 percent in real terms. Government final consumption expenditure grew 4.3 percent, the weakest of the components.

Net exports added to growth in Q1. Real imports of goods and services contracted 1.1 percent while real exports grew 12 percent. In nominal terms imports were stronger, including a 51.5 percent surge in machinery equipment imports, which is consistent with a capital goods cycle rather than a consumption import surge.

Government capital spending was front-loaded and large. Controller General of Accounts data show central capital expenditure of Rs 3.4 lakh crore in April to June against Rs 2.75 lakh crore a year earlier, a rise of roughly 24 percent and about 28 percent of the full-year budget estimate of Rs 12.22 trillion. The Q1 fiscal deficit was Rs 3.1 lakh crore, or 18.2 percent of the budget estimate. Data released Monday for April to July show capital spending of Rs 4.51 trillion, up 30 percent year on year and 37 percent of the annual target, with the fiscal deficit at 27 percent of budget estimates, lower than the 30 percent at the same point last year.

The measurement framework changed. This is the first quarterly estimate published fully on the new 2022-23 base series, incorporating a new output Producer Price Index, a new IIP series, a Banking Services Price Index, and a double-deflation approach for manufacturing GVA in which output and intermediate consumption are deflated separately. Alongside Monday's release, MoSPI published revised annual estimates for FY23 through FY25 and updated quarterly estimates from Q1 FY23 through Q4 FY26.

The comparison base moved. Q1 FY2025-26 was reported at 7.8 percent under the old series and stands at 6.9 percent under the new one. Q4 FY2025-26 stands at 8.6 percent, so this quarter is a sequential deceleration.

Wholesale inflation is running near double digits. WPI inflation was 9.78 percent in July, easing marginally from 9.87 percent in June, which was the highest reading in the new series. Fuel and power inflation was 20.05 percent, manufactured products 8.29 percent, primary articles 8.52 percent. Chemicals ran at 13.12 percent, basic metals 12.56 percent and electrical equipment 12.34 percent. Retail inflation was 4.45 percent in July, the highest since December 2024, with food at 5.52 percent.

Where India genuinely performed well, and it should be said plainly

A 34-percent-of-GDP nominal investment rate, up from 31.4 percent a year earlier, is a real structural improvement and not a statistical artefact of any one deflator. Manufacturing above 9 percent for a second consecutive quarter, in a period that included a crude oil shock and elevated input costs, is a genuine result. Exports growing 12 percent in real terms while the country was absorbing a 50-percent United States tariff regime for part of the preceding year, and while global trade routes were disrupted, is a stronger outcome than most forecasters expected. Merchandise exports separately hit a record $44.24 billion in July, up 19.6 percent year on year.

Consumption at 7.1 percent is healthy by any historical standard for India, even if it trails GVA growth. And the fiscal position improved rather than deteriorated while capex rose, which is an unusual combination and reflects strong corporate tax and customs collections offsetting a 23 percent fall in excise duty after the late-March cut in special additional excise duty on fuel.

The macro-financial management also deserves credit on its own terms. Facing rupee pressure after the West Asia escalation, the RBI chose not to defend the currency with the policy rate, holding the repo at 5.25 percent for a fourth consecutive meeting, and instead used a concessional swap window for FCNR(B) deposits that mobilised about $36.7 billion by July 31, with associated measures taking total inflows past $41 billion. Foreign exchange reserves stood at $692.9 billion on July 31, more than ten months of import cover, and the rupee gained about 1.2 percent in the final week of July.

What is a reasonable interpretation, and should be read as interpretation

The deflator gap is the most substantive analytical question in this release. Nominal GDP grew 10.3 percent and real GDP 7.8 percent. That is a gap of 2.5 percentage points between two growth rates, but the mathematically implied GDP deflator growth is about 2.3 percent, which is the correct figure to compare against price indices. An implied deflator near 2.3 percent sits well below CPI at 4.45 percent and far below WPI near 9.8 percent.

This is not evidence of manipulation, and it should not be presented as such. MoSPI itself addressed the mechanism in Monday's press note: under double deflation, the implicit price deflator for manufacturing GVA can fall or even turn negative when input prices such as crude petroleum and raw materials rise faster than output prices, because value added is the residual between the two. That is precisely the condition India experienced this quarter, with fuel and power wholesale inflation above 20 percent. The ministry noted that other countries have seen the same effect during supply-chain shocks.

The series is better and less comparable at the same time — anyone treating 9.2 percent manufacturing growth as strictly comparable to pre-2026 prints is making an error, and anyone calling the number fabricated is making a different and larger one.

The reasonable interpretation is therefore twofold and should be held together. Double deflation is the internationally recommended method and its adoption improves Indian statistics. It also means that in a quarter of severe input-cost inflation, the method can flatter real manufacturing GVA growth relative to what a single-deflator series would have shown. Both statements are true. A full assessment waits for MoSPI's Sources and Methods publication, due in September.

Government capex mattered, but it cannot be the whole investment story. Nominal gross fixed capital formation at 34.3 percent of an Rs 88.27 lakh crore economy is roughly Rs 30 lakh crore for the quarter. Central government capital expenditure of Rs 3.4 lakh crore is about 11 percent of that. Even adding state governments and central public sector enterprises, the majority of capital formation in the quarter was private, corporate and household. The front-loading of public capex plausibly supported and crowded in the cycle, and Bank of Baroda's Madan Sabnavis attributes much of the private component to data centres, power and metals. But a claim that the quarter was primarily state-financed does not survive the arithmetic.

The base revision flatters the headline comparison. A year-ago quarter revised from 7.8 percent to 6.9 percent mechanically raises this year's year-on-year figure. This is a normal consequence of rebasing and not a criticism of MoSPI. It is a reason to weight the sequential picture, where 7.8 percent follows 8.6 percent, alongside the year-on-year one.

What is genuinely open, and should not be presented as settled

Whether this growth is translating into employment. The available evidence does not settle this, and this piece will not pretend otherwise.

Within the GDP quarter itself, the monthly labour force bulletins showed unemployment rising from 5.2 percent in April to 5.5 percent in May and 5.5 percent in June, with labour force participation falling to 54.4 percent in June from 55 percent in April. Youth unemployment for the 15 to 29 age group was 16.2 percent in June, up from 13.8 percent in April 2025, with female youth unemployment near 20.7 percent against 14.6 percent for young men.

After the quarter closed, the picture improved. July unemployment fell to 5.1 percent, participation rose to 55.4 percent and the employment rate rose to 52.5 percent, which is the pattern you would expect if employment was expanding rather than people leaving the labour force. That July reading describes July, not April to June, and it should not be used to characterise the GDP quarter in either direction.

These monthly bulletins are also a different instrument from the annual Periodic Labour Force Survey, which uses a longer recall period and put usual-status unemployment at around 3.1 to 3.2 percent for the most recent cycle. The two are not contradictory, but they are not interchangeable either.

What can be said: youth unemployment is high and rose over the past year, and that is a real problem regardless of what GDP does. What cannot be said from this data: that 7.8 percent GDP growth failed to create jobs. Quarterly national accounts contain no employment estimate, and monthly labour bulletins are too noisy over a single quarter to establish a causal link. The proposition that capital-intensive investment in data centres, power and metals generates fewer jobs per rupee than labour-intensive manufacturing is economically standard, but applying it to explain this quarter's specific numbers is a hypothesis, not a finding.

Whether the external position is deteriorating. In Q1 itself, it was not. Exports grew 12 percent in real terms, imports contracted 1.1 percent, and net exports added to GDP growth. The Reserve Bank has not yet published balance of payments data for the quarter, so the current account outcome for April to June is unknown as of publication.

The risks are forward-looking and real. India imports roughly 85 percent of its crude. The escalation involving the United States, Israel and Iran in early 2026 and disruption around the Strait of Hormuz took Brent from about $80 to $120 within days, and prices have stayed elevated. Ambit Capital projects oil imports rising 41 percent year on year in FY2027 and the current account deficit widening to roughly 2.9 percent of GDP from 0.7 percent in FY2026. That is a forecast, not an outcome, and it is at the pessimistic end of the range. Foreign portfolio investors withdrew about $21.6 billion between March and April 2026, and the rupee depreciated close to 9 percent over FY2026 before stabilising.

The distinction matters: Q1 exported and imported its way to a positive net trade contribution. The oil bill is a Q2 and Q3 problem, and it is a serious one.

The clearest weakness, and the clearest risk

Agriculture is the weakest line in the release, and the risk is ahead of it. The primary sector grew 2.9 percent and agriculture 3.6 percent, both well below the economy-wide rate.

The monsoon opened at 60 percent of the long period average in June, recovered to 103 percent in July, and stood at a 13 percent seasonal deficit as of August 26. Live storage across 166 major reservoirs was 44.39 percent of capacity on August 1. The India Meteorological Department has forecast below-normal rainfall for August and for the August to September period, with El Niño conditions strengthening. August alone accounts for roughly 29 percent of monsoon rainfall and about 20 percent of kharif sowing.

Kharif acreage is running about 1.6 million hectares below last year. At end-July the shortfall was concentrated in Maharashtra, down 8 percent, Karnataka, down 26 percent, and Odisha, down 12 percent, states that together account for roughly a quarter of national sowing. Input costs compounded it: urea has been imported above $950 a tonne against $400 to $450 before the Iran conflict. Sugar moved from Rs 48 to Rs 55 between July 20 and August 20, prompting duty-free raw sugar imports.

A weak kharif hits Q2 and Q3 output directly, pushes food inflation higher, and squeezes rural demand at exactly the point when the RBI has less room to cut. The central bank projects average FY2027 inflation near 5 percent with a Q3 peak around 5.9 percent, and those forecasts assume a monsoon outcome that is now in doubt.

Inflation persistence is the second risk. Wholesale inflation near 10 percent has not yet passed through to retail in full. ICRA and PL Capital analysts note that WPI does not translate mechanically into CPI, but sustained input-cost pressure of this magnitude usually shows up eventually. If it does, the RBI's neutral stance becomes harder to hold.

What to watch

Chief Economic Adviser V. Anantha Nageswaran told reporters that domestic demand and investment remain the anchors while food prices, monsoon progression and global uncertainty deserve monitoring. That is a fair statement of the risk map rather than a defensive one.

Forecasters are split, and the split is informative. Sabnavis at Bank of Baroda and Devendra Pant at India Ratings both see FY2027 growth crossing 7 percent. The RBI projects 6.7 percent, as does Morgan Stanley. The gap is essentially a disagreement over whether investment can sustain Q1 intensity for three more quarters while crude stays expensive and the kharif underperforms. ICRA's Aditi Nayar separately expects the fiscal deficit to overshoot the budget estimate by Rs 0.9 to 1.0 trillion, absorbable through expenditure savings.

Markets did not read the print as a turning point. The Nifty closed below 24,100 on the day, with the Sensex down more than 300 points.

Three questions decide the year. Whether August and September rainfall spares the kharif crop. Whether the February 2026 interim trade framework with Washington, which cut effective tariffs to roughly 18 percent from 50 percent, is completed, with agriculture still the unresolved chapter. And whether the investment cycle broadens from capital-intensive infrastructure into sectors that absorb more labour per rupee, which is the difference between growth that shows up in national accounts and growth that shows up in household income.

None of that diminishes the quarter. India grew 7.8 percent in a period containing an oil shock, a currency shock, a disrupted shipping corridor and a failed monsoon opening. That is a genuinely strong result. It is also a result whose price measurement deserves scrutiny and whose durability is not yet established, and saying both is not hedging. It is the accurate description.
