Iran and Oman Finalize a Plan for Hormuz. Tehran Says the Strait Stays Shut Anyway.

Iranian and Omani negotiators have settled the technical architecture for managing Persian Gulf shipping. Separately, Iran's top security body has made clear it intends to keep the Strait of Hormuz closed regardless, calling the closure itself a lesson the United States has not yet finished learning.

Sagar Kashyap · 9 Aug 2026

Two stories are unfolding in the Strait of Hormuz right now, and they are easy to mistake for one.

The first is a negotiation between Iran and Oman over how to physically manage traffic through the waterway once it reopens: which lanes ships use, who inspects them, what fees apply. That negotiation is close to finished.

The second is a decision by Iran's senior leadership that the strait will not reopen at all until the United States pays a political price. That decision has nothing to do with Oman, and nothing in the Oman talks can resolve it. Iran has said so explicitly.

The Iran-Oman deal: what's actually been agreed

The framework replaces the pre-war traffic system entirely. Historically, commercial shipping moved through Hormuz largely via Omani waters, under the freedom-of-navigation principle that governs international straits. The new structure splits the route in two: ships entering the Persian Gulf use a northern corridor near the Iranian coast, under Iranian control; ships exiting use a southern corridor near Oman, administered by Muscat, with exit clearance granted after Iran is notified.

Officials on both sides describe the technical work as effectively done. Iranian officials say the arrangement received leadership signoff in Tehran last week. Gulf officials say the Gulf Cooperation Council has endorsed it. The International Maritime Organization and the United States are expected to be involved in any formal announcement, which has not yet happened.

The one unresolved piece inside this track is money. Iran wants a fee of 5% to 7% of cargo value; Oman has proposed roughly 3%. Both governments call this a service fee rather than a toll, a distinction that matters because UNCLOS bars coastal states from charging tolls on transit passage through international straits, but leaves room for charging for services actually rendered, such as security or environmental protection. Eight maritime shipping associations have written to the UN opposing any fee at all, and the IMO has warned that any toll-like charge would set a dangerous precedent. Iran, in the meantime, has already begun collecting ad hoc payments of up to $2 million per vessel outside any formal agreement.

This is a real negotiation with a real, near-final outcome. On its own terms, it says nothing about when, or whether, the strait reopens.

The separate decision: Iran isn't reopening it, on principle

On Saturday, Mohammad Bagher Zolghadr, secretary of Iran's Supreme National Security Council and a commander in the Revolutionary Guard, published a statement making that separation explicit. The strait, he said, stays closed "until America corrects its behavior." He then listed what correction would require:

1. The United States must never, in any language, threaten Iran or insult the country's national and religious sanctities.

2. The United States must permanently end the war and aggression against Iran and its allies in Lebanon, Palestine, Yemen and Iraq.

3. The United States must lift its naval blockade and withdraw its military forces, naval and air, from around Iran.

4. The United States must fully compensate Iran for damages caused by what the statement calls two wars of aggression.

5. The United States must lift all sanctions on Iran.

6. The United States must unconditionally release Iran's frozen and seized assets.

Zolghadr called these the demands of the Iranian people, invoking 160 days of what he described as continuous presence "on the battlefield and in the streets," and said the council would not back down "whether in war or in negotiations."

Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi had already framed the logic a day earlier, saying plainly that the Oman agreement does not mean the strait reopens, and that reopening is subject to additional conditions including compensation for American violations of the June ceasefire memorandum. Iranian army spokesman Amir Akraminia went further Saturday, telling IRNA that the new arrangement in the strait is "irreversible" and that Washington "has no choice but to accept the existing situation," warning of costs "far greater than those it has faced in the past" if it does not.

Iran is not withholding the strait because a shipping agreement is incomplete. It is withholding the strait as punishment, and as proof, to its own public and to Washington, that five months of American bombing did not force Iranian capitulation.

Strip away the diplomatic language and the message is straightforward. The closure is the lesson. Reopening it for free would erase the lesson.

Why the two tracks can't be merged

This is the point worth sitting with. Oman has no ability to deliver any of Zolghadr's six conditions. It cannot withdraw the US military, cannot lift American sanctions, cannot authorize reparations, cannot free frozen Iranian assets held in Western financial systems. Those are decisions that belong to Washington alone.

Which means the Iran-Oman framework can be finalized, announced, and technically operational, and the strait can remain exactly as closed as it is today. The two negotiations are not stages of the same process. One is a shipping arrangement between two coastal states. The other is a political ultimatum aimed at a third country that isn't at the table.

Iranian officials appear to want both tracks visible at once, for different audiences. The Oman deal is aimed at global energy markets and Gulf neighbors, a signal that Iran is a rational, cooperative manager of the waterway once conditions are met. The six conditions are aimed at Washington and at Iran's own domestic hardliners, a signal that no amount of shipping-lane diplomacy substitutes for a political reckoning.

Washington's response

The United States has rejected both the fee structure and the underlying premise. A US official said any temporary route must operate without impediments, meaning no approvals, no tolls, and no party controlling the lanes. The State Department has indicated an Iranian tolling system would make any deal unfeasible. Vice President JD Vance told Fox News on Saturday that Iran has privately told Washington it has no plans to toll the strait at all, a claim that sits uneasily beside Iran's own parliamentary fee legislation and its ambassador's public statements in Beijing describing service charges as a certainty.

Trump has said there will be no tolls for anyone, then added that if anyone is going to charge a fee, the United States will. Central Command says it has redirected 51 commercial vessels under the naval blockade reimposed on Iranian ports last month. The State Department sanctioned six entities and one individual Saturday over alleged efforts to move Iranian funds through digital assets tied to attacks on shipping.

Behind the public firmness sits a harder problem. Officials familiar with internal Pentagon assessments say the US has expended close to four-fifths of its THAAD interceptor stock and roughly half its Patriot inventory since the war began, a position senior commanders have called dangerously low. Independent estimates from the Center for Strategic and International Studies put remaining Patriot stock below 827 interceptors and describe a rebuild timeline measured in years. Trump has dismissed the reporting as false and called the leaks treasonous; a US official has separately confirmed the reductions.

A country negotiating from six fixed demands has little reason to soften them if it believes the other side's capacity to sustain pressure is running out.

What it means for India

None of the six conditions are things Washington appears willing to grant in the near term, which means Indian planners should treat the strait's closure as a variable measured in months, not days, regardless of how the Oman announcement is timed. India imports the bulk of its crude through Hormuz, and a fee regime landing anywhere between Oman's 3% and Iran's 7% becomes a standing cost on that trade the moment normal traffic resumes, layered on top of, not instead of, the political closure Iran has now made explicit.
