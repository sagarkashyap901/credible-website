What Dharmendra Pradhan Actually Built: A Scorecard of His Five Years at the Education Ministry

Beyond the resignation headlines, Pradhan's 2021 to 2026 tenure produced the deepest structural overhaul of Indian education in three decades. Here is what changed, and who it actually helped.

Sagar Kashyap · 26 Jul 2026

Dharmendra Pradhan took charge of the Education Ministry in July 2021, inheriting a freshly approved National Education Policy that existed mostly on paper. Whatever the final verdict on his resignation, the implementation record beneath it is substantial. Here is what he built, sorted by who it was built for.

School Education

Replaced the 10+2 structure with 5+3+3+4. The old system grouped schooling into two blocks after Class 10. The new structure organizes learning around child development stages: five years of foundational learning (including pre-school), three years preparatory, three years middle, and four years secondary. It brings early childhood education formally into the school system for the first time, a stage previously left largely to private playschools.

NIPUN Bharat Mission. A dedicated push to ensure every child achieves basic reading and arithmetic ability by the end of Class 3. This targets what education researchers call India's "learning poverty" problem: children who move up grades without ever mastering the basics, then fall permanently behind.

PM SHRI Schools. 13,076 schools upgraded across 33 states and union territories as of December 2025, backed by a ₹27,360 crore outlay (₹18,128 crore from the Centre). Upgrades include smart classrooms, science and computer labs, and better basic infrastructure like playgrounds and furniture, aimed at making government schools competitive with private ones without charging families private-school fees.

National Curriculum Framework, rolled out to align textbooks and teaching methods with NEP's goals, shifting emphasis toward competency-based learning (can a student apply a concept) rather than rote memorisation (can a student recall a fact).

Mother tongue and regional language instruction in early grades. Foundational learning in a child's home language, with a gradual transition to other languages later, a change grounded in global research showing children grasp new concepts faster when first taught in a familiar language.

NISHTHA teacher training programme, aimed at retraining lakhs of government school teachers in updated pedagogy, since curriculum reform on paper only reaches classrooms if teachers are equipped to deliver it.

DIKSHA and other digital learning platforms expanded, giving teachers and students free access to curriculum-linked digital content in multiple Indian languages, useful both for remote and under-resourced schools.

Higher Education

CUET (Common University Entrance Test). A single entrance exam now used for undergraduate admission across central universities, replacing a patchwork of separate university-level tests. This matters most for students from small towns and rural areas, who previously had to travel to and prepare separately for multiple university-specific exams; CUET gives them one shot at multiple institutions.

Academic Bank of Credits (ABC). A digital system, launched by PM Modi on NEP's first anniversary, that lets students bank academic credits earned across institutions and courses, including IITs and IIMs, and redeem them toward a degree later. Credits stay valid for up to seven years.

Multiple Entry and Exit in undergraduate programmes. Students can now leave a degree program after one year with a certificate, after two with a diploma, or complete the full degree, and re-enter later without losing prior credits. This directly addresses a long-standing Indian problem: students who drop out of college for financial or family reasons previously walked away with nothing to show for the years already completed.

Multidisciplinary higher education with flexible subject choices, allowing, for instance, a science student to take a course in economics or the arts, moving away from the rigid single-stream degrees Indian higher education was historically built around.

Engineering and technical education offered in Indian languages, opening technical degrees to students whose English proficiency, not their technical aptitude, was previously the barrier to entry.

Indian Knowledge System (IKS) integrated into higher education curricula, incorporating traditional Indian science, mathematics, medicine, and philosophy alongside conventional coursework.

Research, innovation, and startup culture pushed inside universities, including incubation support and funding mechanisms intended to make Indian campuses producers of research and enterprise, not just degree-granting institutions.

Foreign university campuses permitted on Indian soil. Under UGC's new framework, 15 global universities have been approved to open campuses in India, including Deakin University and the University of Wollongong (GIFT City, Gujarat), the University of Southampton (Gurugram), and the University of Liverpool, Western Sydney University, and La Trobe University (Bengaluru), among others. For Indian students, this means access to foreign degrees and faculty without the cost of studying abroad, and a step toward reversing the outbound flow of students and the money that goes with them.

The Net Effect

Taken together, these reforms move Indian education on three fronts at once: earlier and more equitable access at the school level, more flexible and forgiving pathways through higher education, and a more internationally connected system that no longer requires ambition to come with an air ticket. Whether the execution matches the intent, especially at the state level, where implementation quality varies sharply, remains the open question. But the policy architecture itself represents the most significant redesign of how India teaches its 250 million school-age children since independence.
